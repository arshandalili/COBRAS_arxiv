\section{Log-density-ratio objectives for activation steering}
\label{apx:ldr-formalize}

This appendix relates \Cobras and existing activation steering methods to log-density-ratio objectives. We first show that the steering field of \Cobras reduces to a kernel log-density-ratio field as $\varepsilon\to\infty$, with the contrastive samples and evaluation bandwidth fixed. We then interpret existing steering directions as log-density-ratio gradients under specified distributional models or fitted estimates.

\subsection{Kernel log-density-ratio limit of \Cobras}
\label{subsec:reduction}

To isolate the contribution of the fitted transport, we examine the unnormalized steering field $\boldsymbol{v}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cobras-field} as the reference weights $\widehat{Q}_{01,ji}$ become uniform, keeping the contrastive samples and evaluation bandwidth $b>0$ fixed.

Consider the limit $\varepsilon\to\infty$ with the contrastive activations and bandwidth $b$ held fixed. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-reference}, the costs $c_{ji}$ vanish and $\widehat{Q}_{01,ji}\to 1/(N_-N_+)$, so the reference no longer favors particular sample pairs, while the proximity kernels $K_b$ remain unchanged. In this limit, the bracketed coefficients in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:local-psi} and~\eqref{eq:local-phi} become common to all desired and all undesired samples, respectively, and hence factor out of the corresponding sums. Because these coefficients are held fixed during differentiation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cobras-field}, they drop out of the logarithmic gradients, leaving a field proportional to the gradient of the log-ratio of the two unweighted kernel sums.
We write the two unweighted kernel estimates as $\hat{p}_b^-(\boldsymbol{x})=N_-^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N_-}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)$ and $\hat{p}_b^+(\boldsymbol{x})=N_+^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_+}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)$. We omit their common kernel normalizing constant, which cancels in the ratio. The limiting field is therefore
\[
\displaystyle
\boldsymbol{v}_b^{\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})
=
b\nablaS\log\frac{\hat{p}_b^+(\boldsymbol{x})}{\hat{p}_b^-(\boldsymbol{x})}.
\]

\begin{proposition}[Reduction to the kernel log-density-ratio field]
\label{prop:ldr-limit}
Fix the contrastive sets $\mathcal{D}^-$ and $\mathcal{D}^+$ on $\Sph$ and the bandwidth $b>0$. On the domain where both fields are defined, the unnormalized field $\boldsymbol{v}$ defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cobras-field} converges uniformly to $\boldsymbol{v}_b^{\mathrm{kde}}$ at rate $O(1/\varepsilon)$ as $\varepsilon\to\infty$.
\end{proposition}

Proposition~\ref{prop:ldr-limit} establishes convergence to the kernel log-density-ratio field at fixed contrastive samples and bandwidth. Appendix~\ref{apx:proof-ldr-limit} provides the proof for Proposition~\ref{prop:ldr-limit}. At finite $\varepsilon$, the steering field combines each sample's proximity to the current activation with weights constructed from the endpoint potentials and reference distribution of the discrete Schr\"{o}dinger Bridge. It therefore incorporates the transport between the two activation populations, while the limiting field depends only on their separate kernel density estimates.

\label{apx:sampling-limit}
\noindent\textbf{Sampling limit.}~The reduction above holds for fixed contrastive samples and bandwidth $b$. Relating the resulting field to the population log-density-ratio gradient would additionally require consistency of the density estimates and their Riemannian gradients as the sample sizes grow and the bandwidth shrinks. Establishing this sampling limit for the geodesic kernel used here is outside the scope of our analysis.

\subsection{Interpretations of existing steering methods}
\label{apx:baseline-interpretations}

Having established this limiting connection for \Cobras, we examine how existing steering directions relate to log-density-ratio gradients under particular models or fitted estimates. These correspondences concern steering directions; finite updates and strength controls differ between methods. ODESteer~\citep{zhaoodesteer} develops related interpretations of Euclidean steering methods.

\noindent\textbf{Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA)~\citep{rimsky2024steering}.}~CAA adds a fixed vector given by the empirical difference between desired and undesired activation means. Modeling the two activation distributions as Gaussians with identity covariance, $\pplus=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_+,\boldsymbol{I}_d)$ and $\pminus=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_-,\boldsymbol{I}_d)$, gives
\[
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\log\frac{\pplus(\boldsymbol{x})}{\pminus(\boldsymbol{x})}
=-(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_+)+(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_-)
=\boldsymbol{\mu}_+-\boldsymbol{\mu}_-.
\]
Estimating the means from $\mathcal{D}^+$ and $\mathcal{D}^-$ therefore recovers the CAA direction under this Gaussian model.

\noindent\textbf{Spherical Steering (SS)~\citep{you2026spherical}.}~For spherical steering, the corresponding gradient is taken on $\Sph$. To interpret its direction, consider a von Mises--Fisher (vMF) model for the desired activations, $\pplus(\boldsymbol{x})\propto e^{\kappa\cos(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu})}$, and a uniform model for the undesired activations, $\pminus=\operatorname{Unif}(\Sph)$. Here $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is the unit contrastive axis defined in Section~\ref{subsec:strength}, and $\kappa>0$ is the concentration parameter. Under this model, the log-density-ratio gradient satisfies
\[
\nablaS\log\frac{\pplus(\boldsymbol{x})}{\pminus(\boldsymbol{x})}
=\kappa\nablaS\cos(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu})
\propto\Pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{\mu}.
\]
Where nonzero, this gradient points along the geodesic toward the desired direction $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, matching the direction of Spherical Steering's norm-preserving rotation. Its finite rotation angle is determined separately by a vMF-based confidence gate.

\noindent\textbf{Inference-Time Intervention (ITI)~\citep{li2023inference}.}~The probe-weight variant of ITI obtains its direction from a linear logistic classifier. For a probe $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\operatorname{sigmoid}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})$, the class-prior correction gives the estimated log-density-ratio
\[
\log\!\left[\frac{N_-}{N_+}\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})}{1-p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})}\right]
=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}+\log\frac{N_-}{N_+}.
\]
Its Euclidean gradient is $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, giving the probe-weight intervention direction. An intercept would add a constant without changing this gradient. ITI also considers a mean-difference intervention, used in its main experiments, to which the Gaussian interpretation given for CAA applies.

\noindent\textbf{Householder pseudo-rotation (HPR)~\citep{pham2024householder}.}~HPR uses a linear probe without an intercept to identify undesired activations and constructs the Householder reflection matrix $\boldsymbol{H}=\boldsymbol{I}-2\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}$, where $\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{\theta}/\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|$ is the unit normal to the probe's decision boundary. For $\boldsymbol{x}\in\Sph$, projecting the reflection displacement onto the tangent space gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x})
&=-2(\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\Pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{u}\\
&=-\frac{2\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|}
\nablaS(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}).
\end{aligned}
\]
On the undesired side of the probe boundary, $\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}<0$, so the multiplier is positive. The projected displacement therefore aligns with the Riemannian gradient of the probe's estimated log-density-ratio whenever that gradient is nonzero. This is a local directional correspondence; HPR's finite update additionally uses a learned rotation angle.

\noindent\textbf{ODESteer~\citep{zhaoodesteer}.}~ODESteer extends the classifier-based construction to nonlinear features and recomputes the steering direction over multiple integration steps. It parameterizes the estimated log-density-ratio as $\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})+\beta$, where $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ is a polynomial count-sketch feature map and $\boldsymbol{w}$ and $\beta$ are fitted coefficients and an intercept. The gradient is $\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}$, where $\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}$ is the Jacobian of the feature map. Normalizing this gradient gives the steering direction
\[
\frac{\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}}
{\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}\|}.
\]
ODESteer integrates the corresponding ODE using $K$ Euler steps over an integration horizon $T$, evaluating the direction at each current activation.

\section{Derivations and Technical Proofs}
\label{apx:theory}

This appendix derives the probability-flow ODE, the Sinkhorn updates for the discrete endpoint potentials, and the weighted tangent-vector expression for the steering field. It then proves the reduction to a kernel log-density-ratio field for fixed contrastive samples and bandwidth.

\subsection{Probability-flow ODE of the continuous bridge}
\label{apx:static}

We derive the probability-flow ODE in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ldr} from the optimal Schr\"{o}dinger Bridge. Assume that $\pminus$ and $\pplus$ are smooth, strictly positive probability densities with respect to spherical volume, and that the reference trajectory distribution $Q$ is Brownian motion on $\Sph$ with diffusion scale $\sigma^2>0$ and a uniform initial distribution. These sufficient conditions ensure that the bridge admits the factorized representation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sb-factorization}~\citep{leonard2013survey}.

The forward propagation of $\varphi(\cdot,0)$ and backward propagation of $\psi(\cdot,1)$ under the reference Brownian dynamics, described in Section~\ref{subsec:sb}, are expressed by the conditional expectations
\[
\begin{aligned}
\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)
&=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\left[
\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}_0,0)
\,\middle|\,
\boldsymbol{x}_t=\boldsymbol{x}
\right],\\
\psi(\boldsymbol{x},t)
&=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\left[
\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_1,1)
\,\middle|\,
\boldsymbol{x}_t=\boldsymbol{x}
\right].
\end{aligned}
\]
With uniform reference marginals, the bridge's activation density satisfies $P_t(\boldsymbol{x})\propto\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)\psi(\boldsymbol{x},t)$, as stated in Eq.~\eqref{eq:product}~\citep{leonard2013survey}.

To obtain deterministic trajectories with these activation densities, we next construct the probability-flow ODE. The bridge has forward stochastic drift $\sigma^2\nablaS\log\psi(\boldsymbol{x},t)$ and diffusion scale $\sigma^2$~\citep{leonard2013survey}. The drift of the probability-flow ODE is obtained by subtracting $\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\nablaS\log P_t$ from this stochastic drift, preserving the evolution of the activation densities~\citep{song2020score, de2022riemannian}. Substituting the stochastic drift and the product representation of $P_t$ yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_t}{\mathrm{d}t}
&=\sigma^2\nablaS\log\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)
-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\nablaS\log P_t(\boldsymbol{x}_t)\\
&=\frac{\sigma^2}{2}
\left[
\nablaS\log\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)
-\nablaS\log\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)
\right]\\
&=\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\nablaS
\log\frac{\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)}
{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Initialized with $\boldsymbol{x}_0\sim\pminus$, the ODE solutions have activation distribution $P_t$ at each $t\in[0,1]$, with $P_1=\pplus$, establishing Eq.~\eqref{eq:ldr}. The next subsection justifies the reduction from the trajectory optimization to the static problem in Eq.~\eqref{eq:static-sb} and gives the updates used to fit the discrete endpoint potentials.

\subsection{Static reduction and Sinkhorn updates}
The reduction from the trajectory optimization in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sb} to the static problem in Eq.~\eqref{eq:static-sb} follows from the decomposition of KL divergence. Let $P_{\mid01}$ and $Q_{\mid01}$ denote the conditional distributions of trajectories given their starting and ending activations under $P$ and $Q$, respectively. Then~\citep{leonard2013survey}
\begin{equation}
\KL(P\,\Vert\,Q)
=\KL(P_{01}\,\Vert\,Q_{01})
+\mathbb{E}_{P_{01}}\!\left[\KL(P_{\mid01}\,\Vert\,Q_{\mid01})\right].
\label{eq:apx-disint}
\end{equation}
The expectation is over starting and ending activations drawn from $P_{01}$. For a fixed joint endpoint distribution $P_{01}$, the second term is nonnegative and becomes zero when $P_{\mid01}=Q_{\mid01}$. Minimizing over trajectory distributions therefore leaves only $\KL(P_{01}\,\Vert\,Q_{01})$ to minimize under the endpoint constraints, giving the static problem.

For the discrete problem, we fit the endpoint potentials using the Sinkhorn algorithm~\citep{cuturi2013sinkhorn}. Substituting the factorization in Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-factors} into the empirical marginal constraints gives
\[
\widehat{\varphi}_{0,j}\sum_i\widehat{\psi}_{1,i}\widehat{Q}_{01,ji}=\frac{1}{N_-},
\qquad
\widehat{\psi}_{1,i}\sum_j\widehat{\varphi}_{0,j}\widehat{Q}_{01,ji}=\frac{1}{N_+}.
\]
Solving each constraint for its endpoint potential while holding the other potential fixed gives the alternating updates, written in log space as
\begin{align}
\log\widehat{\varphi}_{0,j}
&\leftarrow-\log N_- -\log\!\left(\sum_i\exp\!\left[\log\widehat{\psi}_{1,i}+\log\widehat{Q}_{01,ji}\right]\right),\nonumber\\
\log\widehat{\psi}_{1,i}
&\leftarrow-\log N_+ -\log\!\left(\sum_j\exp\!\left[\log\widehat{\varphi}_{0,j}+\log\widehat{Q}_{01,ji}\right]\right).
\label{eq:sinkhorn}
\end{align}
Each sweep updates $\widehat{\varphi}_{0,j}$ first, then uses these updated values to compute $\widehat{\psi}_{1,i}$. At convergence, the reconstructed joint endpoint distribution satisfies both empirical marginal constraints.

\subsection{Derivation of the steering field}
\label{app:grad-psi}

We derive the weighted tangent-vector expression for the steering field in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cobras-field} at a fixed bandwidth $b>0$.

Using $\nablaS d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h})^2=-2\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{h})$, differentiating the kernel in Eq.~\eqref{eq:kernel} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
b\nablaS K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h})
&=-\frac{1}{2}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h})\nablaS d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h})^2\\
&=K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h})\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{h}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:grad-psi}
\end{equation}

Holding the bracketed coefficients in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:local-psi} and~\eqref{eq:local-phi} fixed, we apply Eq.~\eqref{eq:grad-psi} to the outer kernels in $\widetilde{\psi}_b$ and $\widetilde{\varphi}_b$. Taking the logarithmic gradients divides each differentiated sum by the original sum, giving the normalized sample weights
\begin{align}
w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})
&=\frac{\left[\sum_j\widehat{\varphi}_{0,j}\widehat{Q}_{01,ji}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)\right]K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)}
{\widetilde{\psi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})},
\label{eq:weights-pos}\\
w_j^-(\boldsymbol{x})
&=\frac{\left[\sum_i\widehat{\psi}_{1,i}\widehat{Q}_{01,ji}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)\right]K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)}
{\widetilde{\varphi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})}.
\label{eq:weights-neg}
\end{align}
These weights are positive and sum to one within each population. Substituting them into Eq.~\eqref{eq:cobras-field} yields
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})
=\underbrace{\sum_i w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)}_{\text{attraction}}
-\underbrace{\sum_j w_j^-(\boldsymbol{x})\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)}_{\text{repulsion}}.
\label{eq:weighted-cobras-field}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Full gradient of the log-ratio.}~The bracketed coefficients depend on the current activation. Differentiating them in addition to the outer kernels would give
\label{apx:omitted-term}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
b\nablaS\log\frac{\widetilde{\psi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})}{\widetilde{\varphi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})}
={}&\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
&+b\sum_i w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})\nablaS\log\!\left[\sum_j\widehat{\varphi}_{0,j}\widehat{Q}_{01,ji}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)\right]\\
&-b\sum_j w_j^-(\boldsymbol{x})\nablaS\log\!\left[\sum_i\widehat{\psi}_{1,i}\widehat{Q}_{01,ji}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:apx-omitted}
\end{equation}
The steering field defined in Section~\ref{subsec:estimation} holds the bracketed coefficients fixed during differentiation, so these two additional terms are absent. The coefficients and sample weights are recomputed at each activation using the fixed endpoint potentials.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:ldr-limit}}
\label{apx:proof-ldr-limit}

\begin{proof}
Fix the contrastive sets $\mathcal{D}^-$ and $\mathcal{D}^+$ and the bandwidth $b>0$. On the domain of Proposition~\ref{prop:ldr-limit}, the fields $\boldsymbol{v}$ and $\boldsymbol{v}_b^{\mathrm{kde}}$ combine the same tangent vectors with different weights. We therefore bound their difference by comparing these weights. For the kernel log-density-ratio field, define
\[
\begin{aligned}
w_i^{+,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})
&=\frac{K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)}
{\sum_{i'=1}^{N_+}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_{i'}^+)},\\
w_j^{-,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})
&=\frac{K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)}
{\sum_{j'=1}^{N_-}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_{j'}^-)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Differentiating the kernel log-density-ratio and using $b\nablaS K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h})=K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h})\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{h})$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:grad-psi} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol{v}_b^{\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})
&=b\frac{\sum_i\nablaS K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)}{\sum_i K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)}
-b\frac{\sum_j\nablaS K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)}{\sum_j K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)}\\
&=\sum_i w_i^{+,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)
-\sum_j w_j^{-,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{h}_j^-).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:weighted-kde-field}
\end{equation}

To bound the sample weights in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:weights-pos} and~\eqref{eq:weights-neg}, let $c_{\max}=\max_{j,i}c_{ji}$ and $Z=\sum_{j,i}e^{-c_{ji}}$, and define
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\varphi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_j\widehat{\varphi}_{0,j}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-),
\qquad
\widehat{\psi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_i\widehat{\psi}_{1,i}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+).
\label{eq:kernel-sums-general}
\end{equation}

Since $\widehat{Q}_{01,ji}=Z^{-1}e^{-c_{ji}}$ and $0\le c_{ji}\le c_{\max}$,
\[
\textcolor{RoyalBlue}{e^{-c_{\max}}Z^{-1}\widehat{\varphi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})}
\le\sum_j\widehat{\varphi}_{0,j}\widehat{Q}_{01,ji}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)
\le\textcolor{BurntOrange}{Z^{-1}\widehat{\varphi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})}.
\]
Multiplying these inequalities by $K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)$ and summing over $i$ gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\textcolor{ForestGreen}{e^{-c_{\max}}Z^{-1}\widehat{\varphi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})\sum_iK_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)}
&\le\widetilde{\psi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})\\
&\le\textcolor{Plum}{Z^{-1}\widehat{\varphi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})\sum_iK_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Using the \textcolor{RoyalBlue}{lower bound on the numerator} and the \textcolor{Plum}{upper bound on the denominator} gives
\[
w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})
\ge
\frac{
\textcolor{RoyalBlue}{e^{-c_{\max}}Z^{-1}\widehat{\varphi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)
}{
\textcolor{Plum}{Z^{-1}\widehat{\varphi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})\sum_{i'}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_{i'}^+)}
}
=e^{-c_{\max}}w_i^{+,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x}).
\]
Using the \textcolor{BurntOrange}{upper bound on the numerator} and the \textcolor{ForestGreen}{lower bound on the denominator} gives
\[
w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})
\le
\frac{
\textcolor{BurntOrange}{Z^{-1}\widehat{\varphi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)
}{
\textcolor{ForestGreen}{e^{-c_{\max}}Z^{-1}\widehat{\varphi}_b(\boldsymbol{x})\sum_{i'}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_{i'}^+)}
}
=e^{c_{\max}}w_i^{+,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x}).
\]
Dividing by the positive KDE weight yields
\[
e^{-c_{\max}}
\le\frac{w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})}{w_i^{+,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})}
\le e^{c_{\max}}.
\]
The ratio bound implies $|w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})/w_i^{+,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})-1|\le e^{c_{\max}}-1$. Since the KDE weights sum to one,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_i|w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})-w_i^{+,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})|
&=\sum_i w_i^{+,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})\left|\frac{w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})}{w_i^{+,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})}-1\right|\\
&\le e^{c_{\max}}-1.
\end{aligned}
\]
The same calculation for the undesired weights gives $e^{-c_{\max}}\le w_j^-(\boldsymbol{x})/w_j^{-,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})\le e^{c_{\max}}$ and hence $\sum_j|w_j^-(\boldsymbol{x})-w_j^{-,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})|\le e^{c_{\max}}-1$.

Subtracting the two fields gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{v}_b^{\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})
&=\sum_i\bigl(w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})-w_i^{+,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr)\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)\\
&\quad-\sum_j\bigl(w_j^-(\boldsymbol{x})-w_j^{-,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr)\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{h}_j^-).
\end{aligned}
\]
Since geodesic distances are at most $\pi R$, each tangent vector has norm at most $\pi R$ and $c_{\max}\le\pi^2R^2/(2\varepsilon)$. The triangle inequality and the weight bounds therefore yield
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\|\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{v}_b^{\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})\|
&\le\pi R\left(\sum_i|w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})-w_i^{+,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})|
+\sum_j|w_j^-(\boldsymbol{x})-w_j^{-,\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})|\right)\\
&\le2\pi R(e^{c_{\max}}-1)\\
&\le2\pi R\left(e^{\pi^2R^2/(2\varepsilon)}-1\right)
=O(1/\varepsilon).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:prop1-bound}
\end{equation}
The bound is independent of $\boldsymbol{x}$, so the convergence is uniform as $\varepsilon\to\infty$.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:ldr-limit} establishes the kernel log-density-ratio field as a limiting case of the steering field in \Cobras. At finite $\varepsilon$, the sample weights also incorporate the fitted Schr\"{o}dinger Bridge, allowing the steering field to reflect transport between the two populations beyond their separate kernel density estimates.

\section{Nearest-neighbor support gating}
\label{apx:knn}

In this section, we describe a kNN gate that controls steering strength using distances to undesired contrastive samples. We relate this gate to the support score in Eq.~\eqref{eq:support-score}, evaluate its use across steering methods, and examine its sensitivity to calibration.

\subsection{Gate construction and connection to the support score}
\label{apx:knn-gate}

Following nearest-neighbor OOD detection~\citep{sun2022out}, we measure support using the geodesic distance from the prompt activation $\boldsymbol{x}\in\Sph$ to its $k$-th nearest sample in $\mathcal{D}^-$, denoted by $\rho_k(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)$. We calibrate a reference radius using the $\tau$-quantile of these distances within $\mathcal{D}^-$, excluding each sample from its own neighbors, and define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}
&=\operatorname{Quantile}_{\tau}\!\left(
\left\{\rho_k(\boldsymbol{h}_j^-;\mathcal{D}^-\setminus\{\boldsymbol{h}_j^-\})\right\}_{j=1}^{N_-}
\right),\\
g_{\mathrm{kNN}}(\boldsymbol{x})
&=\left[1+\left(\frac{\rho_k(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)}{\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^\gamma\right]^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:knn-gate}
\end{equation}
Here $\tau\in(0,1)$ selects the reference radius at which the gate halves the intervention, and $\gamma>0$ controls the sharpness of attenuation. We fix $\gamma=200$ and use $k=32$ and $\tau=0.98$ unless otherwise specified.

\noindent\textbf{Connection to the support score.}~\label{apx:knn-connection}To connect this distance-based construction to the support score, we examine the kernel sums in Eq.~\eqref{eq:frozen-factors} as $b_g\to0$, holding the contrastive samples and strictly positive endpoint potentials fitted at fixed $\varepsilon$ unchanged.

For the undesired kernel sum, let $j_\star$ index a nearest sample to $\boldsymbol{x}$, so $\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)=d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_{j_\star}^-)=\min_j d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)$. The nearest sample has the largest kernel value by Eq.~\eqref{eq:kernel},
\[
K_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)
\le e^{-\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)^2/(2b_g)}
=K_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_{j_\star}^-).
\]
Keeping the nearest-sample term and replacing its coefficient by the minimum endpoint potential gives a lower bound on $\widehat{\varphi}_{b_g}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:frozen-factors}, while bounding every kernel by the nearest-sample value gives an upper bound:
\[
\textcolor{RoyalBlue}{\left(\min_j \widehat{\varphi}_{0,j}\right)}
e^{-\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)^2/(2b_g)}
\;\le\;\widehat{\varphi}_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})\;\le\;
\textcolor{BurntOrange}{\left(\sum_j \widehat{\varphi}_{0,j}\right)}
e^{-\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)^2/(2b_g)}.
\]
Taking logarithms and multiplying by $-2b_g<0$ reverses the inequalities. Expanding the logarithms gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)^2
\textcolor{BurntOrange}{-2b_g\log\sum_j \widehat{\varphi}_{0,j}}
&\le -2b_g\log\widehat{\varphi}_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
&\le\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)^2
\textcolor{RoyalBlue}{-2b_g\log\min_j \widehat{\varphi}_{0,j}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:knn-kernel-bound}
\end{equation}
Both bounds in Eq.~\eqref{eq:knn-kernel-bound} converge to $\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)^2$ as $b_g\to0$, since the endpoint potentials remain fixed and strictly positive. The enclosed quantity therefore converges to the same value. Applying the same argument to the desired kernel sum, with $\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^+)=\min_i d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)$, gives the two limits
\[
\begin{aligned}
-2b_g\log\widehat{\varphi}_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})
&\longrightarrow\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)^2,\\
-2b_g\log\widehat{\psi}_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})
&\longrightarrow\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^+)^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
Using $u(\boldsymbol{x})=-\log\hat{p}_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})$ and $\hat{p}_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})=\widehat{\varphi}_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})\widehat{\psi}_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:support-score}, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
2b_g u(\boldsymbol{x})
&=-2b_g\log\hat{p}_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
&=-2b_g\log\widehat{\varphi}_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})
-2b_g\log\widehat{\psi}_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
&\longrightarrow\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)^2
+\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^+)^2
\quad\text{as }b_g\to0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:knn-product-limit}
\end{equation}

The small-bandwidth limit thus depends on nearest-sample distances to both populations. The kNN gate uses distance to undesired samples as a surrogate without fitting transport and replaces the nearest sample with the $k$-th neighbor to reduce dependence on an individual sample.

\noindent\textbf{Applying the gate.}~We evaluate the kNN gate once at the last prompt activation, cache its value as $g_{\mathrm{prompt}}$, and reuse it throughout the continuation. For \Cobras, this value replaces $g_{\mathrm{prompt}}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:step} and scales the integration step size. For another method that maps an activation $\boldsymbol{h}$ to $\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}$, the gated output is $\boldsymbol{h}+g_{\mathrm{prompt}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}-\boldsymbol{h})$, so the gate scales the method's displacement. Algorithm~\ref{alg:knn-external} summarizes this external wrapper.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Applying the kNN gate to other steering methods}
\label{alg:knn-external}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A fitted steering method, undesired activations $\mathcal{D}^-\subset\Sph$, layer $\ell$, and gate parameters $k,\tau,\gamma$
\Statex \textbf{Offline calibration}
\State Compute $\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}$ from leave-one-out neighbor distances using Eq.~\eqref{eq:knn-gate}
\Statex \textbf{For each new prompt}
\State Obtain the last prompt activation $\boldsymbol{x}\in\Sph$ at layer $\ell$
\State Compute and cache $g_{\mathrm{prompt}}\gets g_{\mathrm{kNN}}(\boldsymbol{x})$ using Eq.~\eqref{eq:knn-gate}
\For{each token activation $\boldsymbol{h}$ at layer $\ell$, starting with the last prompt token}
    \State Apply the method's steering intervention to obtain $\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}$
    \State Replace $\boldsymbol{h}$ by $\boldsymbol{h}+g_{\mathrm{prompt}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}-\boldsymbol{h})$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Models and generation settings}

Following Zhao~\etal~\cite{zhaoodesteer}, we evaluate all methods on four open-source models: (i) Falcon-7B, \texttt{tiiuae/falcon-7b}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/tiiuae/falcon-7b}}, (ii) Mistral-7B-v0.3, \texttt{mistralai/Mistral-7B-v0.3}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/mistralai/Mistral-7B-v0.3}}, (iii) LLaMA3.1-8B, \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B}}, and (iv) Qwen2.5-7B, \texttt{Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B}}. Unless otherwise specified, generation uses temperature $=0.7$, top-$p=0.9$, and repetition penalty $=1.1$.


\subsection{Datasets}


\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TruthfulQA}~\citep{lin2021truthfulqa} is a benchmark consisting of 817 questions paired with true and false reference answers, along with source information. The questions cover 38 diverse categories and are commonly evaluated in a zero-shot setting. Following Zhao~\etal~\cite{zhaoodesteer}, we construct 5,918 question--answer pairs and use a 40/10/50 train/validation/test split with two-fold cross validation.

    \item \textbf{UltraFeedback}~\citep{cui2023ultrafeedback} is a large-scale alignment dataset covering instruction-following qualities such as truthfulness, honesty, and helpfulness. In our experiments, we use the UltraFeedback-Binarized dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceH4/ultrafeedback_binarized}}. Specifically, we use the preference-modeling split, where each prompt is paired with an accepted response and a rejected response. Following Zhao~\etal~\cite{zhaoodesteer}, we randomly sample 10K training pairs, 500 validation pairs, and 500 test pairs.

    \item \textbf{RealToxicityPrompts}~\citep{gehman2020realtoxicityprompts} is a benchmark for evaluating toxic degeneration in language models. It consists of naturally occurring web prompts paired with toxicity annotations obtained from the Perspective API. The dataset contains approximately 21K toxic prompts with toxicity scores greater than 0.5 and 77K non-toxic prompts. Following Zhao~\etal~\citep{zhaoodesteer}, we train toxicity steering methods using the Jigsaw Unintended Bias in Toxicity Classification dataset\footnote{\url{https://www.kaggle.com/c/jigsaw-unintended-bias-in-toxicity-classification}} and evaluate them on RealToxicityPrompts. Specifically, we construct a balanced Jigsaw training set of 10K sentences by sampling according to toxicity scores, with 5K toxic and 5K benign examples. For evaluation, we select 500 toxic prompts from RealToxicityPrompts and use them as inputs to the LLMs. This setup allows us to assess whether steering methods reduce toxic generations while preserving response quality.

    \item \textbf{TriviaQA}~\citep{joshi2017triviaqa} contains trivia questions with reference answers and evidence documents. We use the modified validation release prepared for ITI transfer evaluation, \href{https://huggingface.co/datasets/OamPatel/iti_trivia_qa_val}{\texttt{OamPatel/iti\_trivia\_qa\_val}}, which supplies accepted answer aliases and one distractor per question.

    \item \textbf{Natural Questions (NQ)}~\citep{kwiatkowski2019natural} contains questions drawn from anonymized Google search queries. We use the modified NQ-Open validation release prepared for ITI transfer evaluation, \href{https://huggingface.co/datasets/OamPatel/iti_nq_open_val}{\texttt{OamPatel/iti\_nq\_open\_val}}. For both NQ and TriviaQA, steering is fitted only on TruthfulQA, and evaluation uses sampled questions with the same prompt format as TruthfulQA. We score the supplied candidates without retrieving evidence documents, using the metric described below.

    \item \textbf{GSM8K}~\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k} contains grade-school math word problems paired with natural-language solutions, with roughly 7.5K training examples and $1{,}319$ test examples. We use questions from the original test split for out-of-distribution evaluation. All methods are fitted on TruthfulQA and evaluated in a 5-shot setting.

    \item \textbf{MMLU}~\citep{hendrycks2020measuring} is a large-scale multiple-choice benchmark covering a broad range of academic and professional subjects. It contains approximately 100K training examples and 14K test examples. We use questions from the default test split for out-of-distribution evaluation and perform 5-shot generation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{appendix:metrics}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Truthfulness (\%).}
    Truthfulness is evaluated on TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2021truthfulqa} using a publicly available fine-tuned LLaMA2-7B judge\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/allenai/truthfulqa-truth-judge-llama2-7B}}. The judge predicts a binary truthfulness label for each generated answer using prompts of the form:
    \[
    \texttt{Q: \{question\} \textbackslash n A: \{answer\}. \textbackslash n True:}
    \]
    We report the percentage of generations classified as truthful.

    \item \textbf{Informativeness (\%).}
    Informativeness is evaluated on TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2021truthfulqa} using a publicly available fine-tuned LLaMA2-7B judge\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/allenai/truthfulqa-info-judge-llama2-7B}}. The judge predicts whether a response is informative or helpful using prompts of the form:
    \[
    \texttt{Q: \{question\} \textbackslash n A: \{answer\}. \textbackslash n Helpful:}
    \]
    We report the percentage of generations classified as informative.

    \item \textbf{Truthfulness $\times$ Informativeness (\%).}
    Following prior work, we report the percentage of responses that are both truthful and informative. For each prompt, this metric is computed as the product of the binary truthfulness and informativeness labels, and the final score is averaged over all prompts.

    \item \textbf{Answer-ranking accuracy (NQ and TriviaQA).}
    Each candidate set contains all accepted reference answers and the supplied distractor. For NQ, the accepted answers come from the \texttt{answer} list, and for TriviaQA, they come from the full \texttt{answer.aliases} list. We score each answer by the sum of its token log-probabilities conditioned on the question prompt, without length normalization. A question is correct when the highest-scoring candidate belongs to the accepted set. Items whose distractor duplicates an accepted answer are retained for all methods and always count as correct.

    \item \textbf{RM$_{\text{mean}}$ and RM$_{90}$.}
    For UltraFeedback, we score helpfulness with \texttt{Skywork/Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/Skywork/Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B}}. We report RM$_{\text{mean}}$, the average reward score across examples and three random seeds, and RM$_{90}$, the 90th percentile reward score.

    \item \textbf{Win Rate (\%).}
    We use these reward scores to compare steered generations with the unsteered model, following RewardBench~\citep{lambert2025rewardbench} and ODESteer~\citep{zhaoodesteer}. For $N$ prompts, let $s_i^A$ and $s_i^B$ denote the scores of responses from systems $A$ and $B$. The win rate of $A$ over $B$ is
    \[
    \mathrm{Win}(A,B)
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \left[
    \mathbbm{1}(s_i^A > s_i^B)
    +
    \frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}(s_i^A = s_i^B)
    \right],
    \]
    where $\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function and ties count as half a win. We report this quantity as a percentage with the unsteered model as $B$, so $50\%$ indicates parity.

    \item \textbf{Toxicity Score.}
    Toxicity is evaluated using the Perspective API\footnote{\url{https://perspectiveapi.com/}}. The toxicity score is a probability-like score indicating the likelihood that a reader would perceive the generated text as toxic. Lower values therefore indicate less toxic generations.

    \item \textbf{Perplexity.}
    We evaluate generation fluency using perplexity under GPT-2-XL~\cite{radford2019language}. Given a generated response $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_T)$, GPT-2-XL assigns a next-token negative log-likelihood
    \[
    \mathcal{L}(y)
    =
    -\frac{1}{T-1}
    \sum_{t=1}^{T-1}
    \log p_{\text{GPT2-XL}}(y_{t+1}\mid y_{\leq t}).
    \]
    The perplexity of $y$ is then,
    $\mathrm{PPL}(y)
    =
    \exp\left(\mathcal{L}(y)\right).$ Lower perplexity indicates that the generated text is more fluent under GPT-2-XL.

    \item \textbf{Dist-$n$.}
    We measure lexical diversity using Dist-$n$ for $n\in\{1,2,3\}$. For a generated response $y$, let $\mathcal{G}_n(y)$ denote the multiset of all word-level $n$-grams in $y$, obtained by whitespace tokenization. Dist-$n$ is defined as
    \[
    \mathrm{Dist}\text{-}n(y)
    =
    \frac{
    \left|\mathrm{Unique}(\mathcal{G}_n(y))\right|
    }{
    \left|\mathcal{G}_n(y)\right|
    }.
    \]
    If the response contains fewer than $n$ words, we set $\mathrm{Dist}\text{-}n(y)=0$. Higher Dist-$n$ indicates greater lexical diversity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Steering implementation and settings}
\label{appendix:implementation_details}
\label{apx:bandwidth}
\label{apx:pseudocode}

We extend the ODESteer codebase\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ZhaoHongjue/odesteer}} to implement the steering methods and baselines.

\noindent\textbf{Steering at inference.}~At each integration step, we select a bandwidth separately for each population:
\begin{equation}
b_\pm(\boldsymbol{x})=
\max\!\left\{\max_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{D}^\pm}
d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h})^2,\ b_{\min}\right\}.
\label{eq:adaptive-bandwidth}
\end{equation}
\textcolor{blue}{We use $b_{\min}=(10^{-3}R)^2$.} Every kernel centered on a desired sample uses $b_+$, including the kernels inside $m^-$. Every kernel centered on an undesired sample uses $b_-$, including those inside $m^+$. The final field remains the difference of weighted log-maps in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cobras-field}, without dividing either branch by its bandwidth. Thus the common-$b$ gradient-ratio identity and Proposition~\ref{prop:ldr-limit} describe the fixed-bandwidth construction. \textcolor{blue}{The support gate uses the separate, fixed bandwidth $b_g=\sigma^2/256$.}

\paragraph{Numerical evaluation.}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:cobras} in Section~\ref{subsec:procedure} gives the adaptive procedure. \textcolor{blue}{Weighted kernel estimates} and normalized weights are evaluated in log space. Implementations also clamp cosines away from $\pm1$ and vector norms away from zero for numerical stability. \textcolor{blue}{Steering acts through a forward hook at layer $\ell$ on the last-token activation at each decoding step, with the prompt gate cached for the continuation.}

\paragraph{\textcolor{blue}{Experimental settings.}}
\textcolor{blue}{Unless varied in an ablation, we use $K=10$ integration steps and $\kappa=20$. The main in-distribution evaluation is ungated, with $g_q=1$ and $T=0.5$. Support-gated OOD evaluation uses $p=0.7$ and $T=0.65$. The component and progressive ablations use the kNN gate, as specified in Appendix~\ref{apx:component-ablations}. Experiments were run using a combination of one H100 80GB GPU and two A100 80GB GPUs.}

\section{\textcolor{blue}{Computational Cost}}
\label{apx:computational-cost}

\textcolor{blue}{\Cobras fits the transport plan once and evaluates the steering field repeatedly during generation. We examine the cost of both stages and assess how reducing the contrastive set or the number of integration steps changes decoding overhead and steering performance.}

\paragraph{\textcolor{blue}{Computational complexity.}}
Transport fitting and reference-score computation are performed offline. With pairwise transport costs and Sinkhorn scalings precomputed, each steered token requires $K$ field evaluations, each costing $O(N_-N_+ + d(N_-+N_+))$. The $N_-N_+$ term comes from combining connections between the contrastive populations, while $d(N_-+N_+)$ covers distances and tangent vectors in activation space. This expression describes steering integration and excludes the language model's forward pass. The support gate is evaluated once per prompt.

\paragraph{\textcolor{blue}{Fitting and memory.}}
\textcolor{blue}{On an A100 80GB GPU in float32, fitting takes approximately $13$ ms for $N_+=1311$ and $N_-=1672$ after initialization. A scaling experiment with synthetic activations and blocked distance computation takes $9.6$ s at $60{,}000$ samples per population. At $100{,}000$ per population, the fit exceeds available memory. The float32 cost matrix alone occupies $37.3$ GiB, illustrating the storage constraint imposed by the pairwise representation.}

\paragraph{\textcolor{blue}{Decoding overhead.}}
\textcolor{blue}{We benchmark LLaMA3.1-8B at layer $13$ on the same GPU in float32, using $100$ prompts, batch size $10$, and $50$ greedy decoding steps per prompt. We time each method in five repeated rounds, discard the first round (to avoid warm-up effects), and report the median runtime over the remaining four. With $N_+=1311$, $N_-=1672$, and $K=10$, the unsteered model takes $62.5$ ms per decoding step for the batch. \Cobras adds $20.2$ ms, giving $82.7$ ms total and approximately $32\%$ overhead.}

\paragraph{\textcolor{blue}{Reducing computational cost.}}
\textcolor{blue}{We compare three approximations. Random subsampling reduces the contrastive set before fitting, fewer integration steps reduce field evaluations per token, and nearest-sample truncation restricts field evaluation to the $64$ closest samples in each population. Table~\ref{tab:runtime-approximations} reports decoding overhead and the corresponding TruthfulQA performance.}

\subsection{Empirical Validation of Spherical Geometry assumption}
\label{appendix:norm_concentration}

\Cobras models residual-stream activations at a fixed layer as lying approximately on a hypersphere. As shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:cobras}, it projects each query onto the fitted sphere (Line~\ref{alg:cobras:norm_equation}), \[ \boldsymbol{x}_0 = R \frac{\boldsymbol{h}_q}{\|\boldsymbol{h}_q\|}, \] performs steering on the sphere, and restores the original norm afterward. Thus, steering depends on activation direction rather than magnitude. We test this assumption by measuring the coefficient of variation (CoV) of residual-stream norms across layers and model families. For the four models used in the main experiments, we use 5,918 TruthfulQA activations; for four additional architectures (Gemma-2-2B~\cite{team2024gemma}, GPT-OSS-20B~\cite{agarwal2025gpt}, LLama-3.2-1B, Qwen2.5-1.5B), we use approximately 110K tokens of web text.

From Table~\ref{tab:norm_concentration}, we observe that norms are least concentrated in the first three layers and substantially more concentrated in later layers for all four models used in our experiments. The same qualitative pattern holds across the additional architectures, although the degree of concentration varies across model families. A notable exception is the initial attention-sink token (e.g., \texttt{<|begin\_of\_text|>} for LLaMA). Although these tokens account for only about 0.45\% of the web-text tokens, their norms can reach up to $418\times$ the median. For example, including them increases the overall CoV of Qwen2.5-1.5B from 0.19 to 9.20. This does not affect \Cobras, since steering is applied to the last prompt token and subsequent generation positions, not the initial token. Overall, these results support the hyperspherical approximation in the layers and positions where \Cobras is applied, while identifying the initial attention-sink token as a clear exception.

Overall, \Cobras preserves lexical diversity close to the original model across all four backbones. The gap is small for Mistral-7B and LLaMA3.1-8B, where Dist-2 and Dist-3 remain nearly unchanged compared to the original model.

We also observe that some baselines reduce diversity more noticeably. For example, CAA lowers diversity on Falcon-7B and Qwen2.5-7B, while SphericalSteer shows a larger drop on Qwen2.5-7B. In contrast, \Cobras maintains high Dist-2 and Dist-3 scores, suggesting that its detoxification effect does not come from generating overly repetitive or generic responses.

\subsection{Performance vs Steering Strength for baselines}

To select the steering strength for each baseline, we sweep over model-specific ranges: $T=20$--$23$ for Falcon-7B, $T=2$--$5$ for Mistral-7B, $T=3$--$6$ for LLaMA3.1-8B, and $T=13$--$16$ for Qwen2.5-7B. Figure~\ref{fig:performance_vs_t} shows the performance of RepE, ITI, CAA, and ODESteer on TruthfulQA and UltraFeedback. For baselines without a comparable steering-strength parameter, we use the best values reported in their papers.

The results show that the best steering strength depends on both the model and the method. In general, some methods improve TruthfulQA performance at moderate values of $T$, but larger steering strengths can hurt UltraFeedback performance. This trend is especially visible for ODESteer on Mistral-7B and LLaMA3.1-8B, where increasing $T$ leads to a large drop in UltraFeedback win rate. CAA is more stable on UltraFeedback and often improves with larger $T$, while ITI tends to change more gradually across the sweep.
