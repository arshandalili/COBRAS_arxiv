\section{A Unified Log-density Ratio Optimization Framework for Activation Steering}
\label{apx:ldr-formalize}

In this section, we show that diverse activation steering methodologies can be unified under a single theoretical framework governed by log-density ratio optimization. Specifically, we analyze how these seemingly disparate techniques implicitly optimize the ratio $\log(p_+ / p_-)$ by reinterpreting them as specific geometric configurations of a continuous global flow. Formally, let $p_{+}$ and $p_{-}$ define a pair of continuous densities corresponding to the contrastive behavior distributions $\mathcal{D}^{+}$ and $\mathcal{D}^{-}$, respectively. Given an initial query activation $\boldsymbol{h}_q$ on an underlying representation manifold $\mathcal{M}$, the steering trajectory is obtained by integrating the continuous-time ordinary differential equation:

\begin{equation}
d\boldsymbol{x}_t = \nabla_{\mathcal{M}}\log\frac{p_{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})}{p_{-}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})}\,dt, \qquad \boldsymbol{x}_{0} = \hquery,
\label{eq:global-flow}
\end{equation}

for $K$ steps over a specified time horizon $[0, T]$. Here, $\boldsymbol{x}_{t}$ denotes the steered activation along the flow, initialized as $\boldsymbol{x}_{0} = \hquery$. By systematically varying the manifold geometry $\mathcal{M}$, the step horizon $K$, and the parameterization of the density functions, we can recover prominent steering paradigms from this single formulation~\footnote{\citet{zhaoodesteer} presents a related unification of several of these methods, but their analysis is confined to Euclidean space; by treating the manifold $\mathcal{M}$ as an explicit design choice, however, our framework additionally captures geometry-aware methods such as SS and HPR.}.

For example, the following methods can be recalled from this perspective as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:steering_framework}.




\paragraph{Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA)~\citep{rimsky2024steering}} The  CAA algorithm can be framed as a specific, single-step optimization instantiation of a continuous global flow over the hidden state manifold. Specifically,  CAA simplifies this trajectory by optimizing over a flat Euclidean space $\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^d$ via a single discrete step ($K=1$). By modeling the factual (positive) and counterfactual (negative) activation distributions as isotropic Gaussians sharing an identity covariance $p_{+}=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}, \boldsymbol{I}_d)$ and $p_{-}=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{-}, \boldsymbol{I}_d)$, the complex continuous gradient field collapses into a constant shift vector defined by the difference of their respective means  $\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}\log\frac{p_{+}}{p_{-}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_+ - \boldsymbol{\mu}_-$. Consequently, the empirical practice of injecting a static difference vector during the forward pass is mathematically grounded as an exact, first-order approximation toward maximizing the target likelihood ratio.

\paragraph{Spherical Steering (SS)~\citep{you2026spherical}}


To relax the assumption of a flat Euclidean space and account for the directional nature of high-dimensional neural representations, Spherical Steering constrains the optimization trajectory to a unit hypersphere manifold, setting $\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{S}^{d-1}$. Under a single-step discrete horizon ($K=1$), the method models the target positive activations using a von Mises-Fisher (vMF) distribution $p_+=\mathrm{vMF}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mu},\kappa)$ which acts as the spherical analogue to an isotropic Gaussian with a mean direction vector $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and a concentration parameter $\kappa$. Conversely, the negative behavior is modeled as a uniform distribution over the sphere $p_-=\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{S})$. The unit direction $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ toward the positive activations is empirically defined by normalizing the difference between the mean positive and negative activations: $$\boldsymbol{\mu}= \frac{\bar{\boldsymbol{h}}^{+} - \bar{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}}{\|\bar{\boldsymbol{h}}^{+} - \bar{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}\|}.$$

When evaluating the global flow framework under these spherical constraints, the Riemannian gradient of the likelihood ratio yields a non-linear direction proportional to the logarithmic map on the sphere $\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\log\tfrac{p_+}{p_-} \propto\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})$ which corresponds to the Slerp direction toward $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ in the direction of this Riemannian gradient. By explicitly respecting the spherical geometry of hidden states, SS ensures that the magnitude of the activation vector is preserved, avoiding out-of-distribution distortion while performing directional steering.

\paragraph{Inference-Time Intervention (ITI) \citep{li2023inference}} Rather than assuming explicit parametric distributions for the hidden states, ITI leverages a discriminative classifier to implicitly estimate the underlying density ratio. Operating within a single-step horizon ($K=1$) over a flat Euclidean vector space $\mathcal{M} = \mathbb{R}^d$, ITI trains a linear logistic probe, $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \boldsymbol{x})$, to distinguish between positive and negative activation vectors. By invoking the classic equivalence between logistic regression and density ratio estimation, the log-density ratio can be rewritten linearly as $\log \frac{p_+(\boldsymbol{x})}{p_-(\boldsymbol{x})} = \boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \boldsymbol{x} + \log \frac{N_-}{N_+}$, where $N_+$ and $N_-$ represent the respective sample counts. Taking the gradient with respect to the manifold yields a constant vector field defined entirely by the probe's learned weights:$$\nabla_{\mathcal{M}} \log \frac{p_+}{p_-} = \boldsymbol{\theta}$$This formulation reveals a powerful geometric justification for ITI: the orthogonal vector to the classifier's decision boundary acts as the exact gradient direction needed to maximize the factual likelihood ratio. Consequently, shifting activations along the direction of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ during inference provides a theoretically grounded, first-order trajectory for target behavior optimization.
\paragraph{Householder pseudo-rotation (HPR)~\citep{pham2024householder}}  To reconcile discriminative, probe-based steering with non-Euclidean representation spaces, the HPR method maps the log-density ratio directly onto a hyperspherical manifold ($\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{S}^{d-1}$) under a single-step horizon ($K=1$). Similar to ITI, the method first projects the log-density ratio linearly using a trained linear probe, $\log \frac{p_+(\boldsymbol{x})}{p_-(\boldsymbol{x})} = \boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \boldsymbol{x} + \log \frac{N_-}{N_+}$. To respect the spherical constraints, HPR constructs an orthogonal Householder reflection matrix, $\boldsymbol{H} = \boldsymbol{I} - 2\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u}^\top$, where $\boldsymbol{u} = \boldsymbol{\theta} / \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|$ represents the unit normal vector to the probe's decision boundary. By reflecting the normalized activation vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}} = \boldsymbol{x} / \|\boldsymbol{x}\|$ across this boundary, the directional gradient on the sphere is formalized as $\nabla_{\mathcal{S}} \log \frac{p_+(\boldsymbol{x})}{p_-(\boldsymbol{x})} \propto \Pi_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}(\boldsymbol{H}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}} - \hat{\boldsymbol{x}})$, where $\Pi_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}$ denotes the projection onto the tangent space at $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}$. Geometrically, this confirms that the optimal trajectory corresponds to a Spherical Linear Interpolation (Slerp) toward the reflected state $\boldsymbol{H}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}$. By leveraging a reflection operator, HPR effectively utilizes the probe's decision boundaries to execute directional steering while intrinsically preserving the latent norm of the activation path.

\paragraph{ODESteer~\citep{zhaoodesteer}} Departing from single-step first-order approximations ($K=1$), ODESteer fully embraces the continuous-time formulation of the global flow framework by integrating across multiple steps ($K > 1$) over a flat Euclidean vector space $\mathcal{M} = \mathbb{R}^d$. Rather than adopting simple parametric distributions or linear probes, this approach models complex, non-linear activation dynamics by parameterizing the log-density ratio with a polynomial count-sketch feature map $\phi$ and setting $\log \frac{p_+(\boldsymbol{x})}{p_-(\boldsymbol{x})} = \boldsymbol{w}^\top \phi(\boldsymbol{x}) + b$, where the parameter pair $(\boldsymbol{w}, b)$ is fit empirically over the contrastive dataset. Under this non-linear projection, the steering direction is dictated by a dynamic, normalized vector field:$$\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \frac{\boldsymbol{J}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})^\top \boldsymbol{w}}{\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})^\top \boldsymbol{w}\|}$$where $\boldsymbol{J}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})$ denotes the Jacobian matrix of the feature map $\phi$ evaluated at the current state $\boldsymbol{x}$. By evaluating and integrating this state-dependent vector field using discrete Euler steps over the time horizon $[0, T]$, ODESteer traces a curved, non-linear path through the representation space. This multi-step integration allows the model to continuously adapt to the changing manifold geometry during the steering process, providing precise control while tracking complex latent boundaries.

\section{Derivations and Implementation}
\label{apx:theory}

\textcolor{blue}{This appendix derives the continuous bridge field, the discrete transport fit, and the local gradient interpretation of the steering field. It then separates the transport limit from the sampling limit needed to recover the population log-density-ratio gradient.}

\subsection{Continuous bridge and discrete transport}
\label{apx:static}

\paragraph{\textcolor{blue}{Probability-flow identity.}}
\textcolor{blue}{Song et al.~\citep[Sec.~4.3, Eq.~13]{song2020score} give the following statement.}
\begin{quote}
\textcolor{blue}{``For all diffusion processes, there exists a corresponding deterministic process whose trajectories share the same marginal probability densities $\{p_t(\boldsymbol{x})\}_{t=0}^{T}$ as the SDE.''}
\end{quote}
\textcolor{blue}{For the Euclidean SDE $\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{w}$, their probability-flow ODE is}
\[
\textcolor{blue}{\displaystyle
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}
=\Big[\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},t)-\frac{1}{2}g(t)^2\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\log p_t(\boldsymbol{x})\Big]\,\mathrm{d}t.
}
\]
\textcolor{blue}{In the source notation, $\boldsymbol{f}$ is the drift, $g(t)$ is the diffusion amplitude, $\boldsymbol{w}$ is standard Brownian motion, and $T$ is the diffusion time horizon.}

\paragraph{\textcolor{blue}{Application to the spherical bridge.}}
\textcolor{blue}{The formula above is stated in Euclidean coordinates. De Bortoli et al.~\citep[App.~D.1]{de2022riemannian} give the corresponding construction for Brownian motion on a Riemannian manifold, using intrinsic gradients and densities with respect to Riemannian volume. Their Appendix~M.3, Eq.~270 includes a drift and is written in reverse time. In forward time on $\Sph$, the probability-flow velocity is $\boldsymbol{u}-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\nablaS\log p_t$ for tangent drift $\boldsymbol{u}$ and constant diffusion variance $\sigma^2$. For smooth positive endpoint densities, the bridge drift is $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\sigma^2\nablaS\log\psi(\boldsymbol{x},t)$~\citep{leonard2013survey}. Combining this drift with $p_t\propto\varphi\psi$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:product} gives}
\[
\textcolor{blue}{\displaystyle
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}_t}{dt}
&=\sigma^2\nablaS\log\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)
-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\nablaS\log\!\left[\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)\right]\\
&=\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\nablaS\log\frac{\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)}{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)}.
\end{aligned}
}
\]
\textcolor{blue}{Initialized from $\pminus$, the deterministic flow has the same activation distributions $P_t$ as the bridge. The resulting velocity is Eq.~\eqref{eq:ldr}.}

\paragraph{Static formulation.}
Conditioning trajectories on their starting and ending activations separates a path law $P$ into its endpoint coupling $P_{01}$ and conditional path laws $P_{\mid01}$. The corresponding relative-entropy decomposition is~\citep{leonard2013survey}
\begin{equation}
\KL(P\,\Vert\,Q)
=\KL(P_{01}\,\Vert\,Q_{01})
+\mathbb{E}_{P_{01}}\!\left[\KL(P_{\mid01}\,\Vert\,Q_{\mid01})\right].
\label{eq:apx-disint}
\end{equation}
For any endpoint coupling, the second term is minimized by retaining the reference bridges. Thus the continuous problem reduces to entropy minimization over endpoint couplings. Atomic empirical endpoints have infinite relative entropy against Brownian endpoints. We therefore discretize the static problem rather than impose empirical measures directly in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sb}.

Choose discrete reference weights proportional to $e^{-c_{ji}}$ and normalize them to sum to one. \textcolor{blue}{Their relative entropy objective over couplings with uniform marginals differs by a constant from the objective in Eq.~\eqref{eq:e-ot}.} The strictly positive kernel gives a unique optimal coupling with the factorization in Eq.~\eqref{eq:plan}. Sinkhorn fitting alternates the log-space updates
\begin{align}
\log a_j^-&=-\log N_- -
\operatorname{logsumexp}_i[\log a_i^+-c_{ji}],\nonumber\\
\log a_i^+&=-\log N_+ -
\operatorname{logsumexp}_j[\log a_j^--c_{ji}].
\label{eq:sinkhorn}
\end{align}
At convergence the plan satisfies both marginals, while the finite number of sweeps used in the implementation gives an approximate fit. The geodesic kernel and subsequent stationary field do not preserve the exact Brownian bridge construction.

\subsection{Kernel gradients and conditional weights}
\label{app:grad-psi}

The weighted logarithm maps in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cobras-field} arise from differentiating kernel sums at a fixed bandwidth $b$. For $\theta=d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h})/R<\pi$, differentiating $R^2\arccos^2(R^{-2}\boldsymbol{x}^\top\boldsymbol{h})$ and projecting onto the tangent space gives
\[
\nablaS d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h})^2
=-\frac{2\theta}{\sin\theta}\Pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{h}
=-2\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{h}).
\]
At $\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{h}$, use the continuous limit, which is zero. For fixed positive $m_i$, the chain rule therefore yields
\begin{equation}
b\nablaS\log\sum_i m_iK_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i)
=\sum_i w_i(\boldsymbol{x})\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{h}_i),
\quad
w_i(\boldsymbol{x})=
\frac{m_iK_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i)}
{\sum_{i'}m_{i'}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_{i'})}.
\label{eq:grad-psi}
\end{equation}
Taking $m_i=a_i^+$ or $m_j=a_j^-$ gives the log-gradients of the fitted kernel factors. Uniform $m_i$ gives the corresponding kernel density-estimate gradient.

\paragraph{Gradient interpretation of the steering field.}
\label{apx:omitted-term}
At the current iterate $\boldsymbol{q}\in\Sph$, evaluate the conditional coefficients $m^+_{i,b}(\boldsymbol{q})$ and $m^-_{j,b}(\boldsymbol{q})$. Let $\boldsymbol{y}\in\Sph$ denote the point at which the kernels are evaluated, and define
\begin{equation}
F_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{y})=
\log\frac{\sum_i m^+_{i,b}(\boldsymbol{q})K_b(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)}
{\sum_j m^-_{j,b}(\boldsymbol{q})K_b(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)}.
\label{eq:local-objective}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:grad-psi} gives $\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{q})=b\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}F_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{y}) |_{\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{q}}$. Differentiation is with respect to $\boldsymbol{y}$, with the coefficients evaluated at $\boldsymbol{q}$ and recomputed at the next iterate.

Using the same variable $\boldsymbol{x}$ in both the coefficients and the kernels gives
\[
\Psi_b^+(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_i m^+_{i,b}(\boldsymbol{x})K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+),
\qquad
\Psi_b^-(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_j m^-_{j,b}(\boldsymbol{x})K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-).
\]
Differentiating their log-ratio yields
\begin{equation}
b\nablaS\log\frac{\Psi_b^+}{\Psi_b^-}
=\boldsymbol{v}
+b\sum_i\bar{w}_i^+\nablaS\log m^+_{i,b}
-b\sum_j\bar{w}_j^-\nablaS\log m^-_{j,b},
\label{eq:apx-omitted}
\end{equation}
with every term evaluated at $\boldsymbol{x}$. The additional terms need not vanish, so this gradient generally differs from $\boldsymbol{v}$.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:ldr-limit}}
\label{apx:proof-ldr-limit}

Because $K_b$ depends only on geodesic distance, its integral over $\Sph$ is independent of the kernel center. The kernel estimates in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ldr-kde} therefore share a normalizing constant, which cancels in their ratio.

Fix the projected contrastive activations and a shared bandwidth $b>0$, and let $c_{\max}=\max_{j,i}c_{ji}$. For any positive scalings in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ext-psi}--\eqref{eq:ext-phi}, the following bound holds away from the contrastive activations' antipodes:
\begin{equation}
\|\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{v}_b^{\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})\|
\le 2\pi R(e^{c_{\max}}-1).
\label{eq:prop1-bound}
\end{equation}

\begin{proof}
Write $S_-(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_j a_j^-K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)$. Since $0\le c_{ji}\le c_{\max}$, every desired multiplier satisfies
\[
e^{-c_{\max}}S_-(\boldsymbol{x})
\le m^+_{i,b}(\boldsymbol{x})\le S_-(\boldsymbol{x}).
\]
Its log-range across $i$ is therefore at most $c_{\max}$, independently of the positive scalings. The undesired multipliers obey the same bound.

For a probability vector $u$, reweighting by positive multipliers of log-range at most $c_{\max}$ gives $w_i=u_i m_i/\sum_k u_km_k$. After a common rescaling, both $m_i$ and their $u$-weighted mean lie in $[1,e^{c_{\max}}]$. Consequently, $\|w-u\|_1\le e^{c_{\max}}-1$. Apply this to the kernel-only weights $u_i^+\propto K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)$ and the conditional weights $\bar{w}_i^+$. Each log-map has norm at most $\pi R$, so
\[
\left\|\sum_i(\bar{w}_i^+-u_i^+)
\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)\right\|
\le\pi R(e^{c_{\max}}-1).
\]
The negative branch contributes the same bound. Equation~\eqref{eq:grad-psi} identifies their kernel-only difference with $\boldsymbol{v}_b^{\mathrm{kde}}$, proving Eq.~\eqref{eq:prop1-bound}. Finally, $c_{\max}\le\pi^2R^2/(2\varepsilon)$ gives uniform convergence and the $O(1/\varepsilon)$ rate at fixed $b$.
\end{proof}

The bound requires only positive scalings and thus also holds after finitely many Sinkhorn iterations. It describes the unnormalized field on a fixed dataset at a shared fixed bandwidth. Normalized directions converge wherever the limiting field's norm is bounded away from zero.

\subsection{Implementation details}
\label{apx:bandwidth}
\label{apx:pseudocode}

\paragraph{Bandwidth selection.}
The implementation selects a squared-distance scale separately for each population at every iterate:
\begin{equation}
b_\pm(\boldsymbol{x})=
\max\!\left\{\max_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{D}^\pm}
d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h})^2,\ b_{\min}\right\}.
\label{eq:adaptive-bandwidth}
\end{equation}
Here $b_{\min}>0$ is a numerical floor. Every kernel centered on a desired sample uses $b_+$, including the kernels inside $m^-$. Every kernel centered on an undesired sample uses $b_-$, including those inside $m^+$. The final field remains the difference of weighted log-maps in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cobras-field}, without dividing either branch by its bandwidth. Thus the common-$b$ gradient-ratio identity and Proposition~\ref{prop:ldr-limit} describe the fixed-bandwidth construction. The gate uses the separate, fixed bandwidth $b_g$.

\paragraph{Numerical evaluation.}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:cobras} in Section~\ref{subsec:procedure} gives the adaptive procedure. Kernel sums and normalized weights are evaluated in log space. Implementations also clamp cosines away from $\pm1$ and vector norms away from zero for numerical stability.

\paragraph{Computational cost.}
Transport fitting and reference-score computation are performed offline. With pairwise transport costs and Sinkhorn scalings precomputed, each steered token requires $K$ field evaluations, each costing $O(N_-N_+ + d(N_-+N_+))$. The $N_-N_+$ term comes from combining connections between the contrastive populations, while $d(N_-+N_+)$ covers distances and tangent vectors in activation space. This expression describes steering integration and excludes the language model's forward pass. The support gate is evaluated once per prompt.

\section{Calibration and Properties of the Support Gate}
\label{apx:ood-gate}

\paragraph{Support score and reference prompts.}
The gate uses the product score in Eq.~\eqref{eq:support-score} at a fixed bandwidth $b_g$. Its two factors summarize weighted proximity to desired and undesired activations, so the score depends on support from both populations. Both reference and query activations are taken at the steering layer and last prompt position, projected onto $\Sph$, and scored identically. This avoids assuming that prompt-only and contrastive question--answer activations have the same score distribution.

In the TruthfulQA gate analysis, $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ref}}$ contains the training-fold prompts and the gate is applied to the held-out fold. For OOD evaluation, it contains all $817$ TruthfulQA prompts. No GSM8K, MMLU, NQ, or TriviaQA prompt is used for calibration.

\paragraph{Interpreting the coverage parameter.}
The parameter $p$ sets the reference rank threshold at or below which $g=1$. If $n$ reference scores and one new score are exchangeable and almost surely distinct, the new score's rank $nr$ is uniform on $\{0,\ldots,n\}$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\Pr(g=1)=\frac{\lfloor np\rfloor+1}{n+1}.
\label{eq:gate-finite-coverage}
\end{equation}
As $n$ grows at fixed $p<1$, this probability tends to $p$, and the mean gate tends to $\int_0^p1\,dr+(1-p)^{-1}\int_p^1(1-r)\,dr=(1+p)/2$. At $p=0.7$, these limits correspond to no gate attenuation on $70\%$ of new in-distribution prompts and a mean gate of $0.85$, under the stated assumptions.

These statements average over both reference and query scores. Our calibration reuses bridge-training prompts, for which exchangeability with new-query scores has not been established. The calculation therefore interprets $p$ under the stated assumptions without providing a coverage guarantee for this fitted gate.

\section{\textcolor{blue}{Nearest-neighbor support gating}}
\label{apx:knn}

\textcolor{blue}{In this appendix, we describe the kNN gate and explain its relation to the product support score. We then compare steering methods under the same gate and examine sensitivity to the calibration parameters.}

\textcolor{blue}{The bridge formulation motivates a steering direction through the potential ratio and a support score through their product. In \Cobras, the steering weights and product score use the same fitted transport scalings. The kNN gate provides a distance-based surrogate for support control, following nearest-neighbor OOD detection~\citep{sun2022out}. The connection to the product score follows from the small-bandwidth behavior of its kernel factors, derived below. Because the gate requires only activation distances, it can also scale interventions from other steering methods.}

\subsection{\textcolor{blue}{Gate construction and connection to the product score}}
\label{apx:knn-gate}

Let $\rho_k(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)$ be the geodesic distance from $\boldsymbol{x}\in\Sph$ to its $k$-th nearest sample in $\mathcal{D}^-$. We set a reference radius from the within-population distances, excluding each sample from its own neighbors, and define the gate
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}
&=\operatorname{Quantile}_{\tau}\!\left(
\left\{\rho_k(\boldsymbol{h}_j^-;\mathcal{D}^-\setminus\{\boldsymbol{h}_j^-\})\right\}_{j=1}^{N_-}
\right),\\
g_{\mathrm{kNN}}(\boldsymbol{x})
&=\left[1+\left(\frac{\rho_k(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)}{\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^\gamma\right]^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:knn-gate}
\end{equation}
The gate equals $1/2$ at $\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and decreases smoothly as the query moves away from the undesired samples. The quantile $\tau\in(0,1)$ therefore sets the radius at which steering is halved, and $\gamma>0$ sets how sharply it falls. By contrast, the coverage $p$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gate} sets the reference rank up to which steering keeps full strength. We use $k=32$ and $\tau=0.98$, with $\gamma=50$ in Table~\ref{tab:abstain-results} and $\gamma=200$ in Table~\ref{tab:knn-external}.

As with $g_q$, the distance gate is evaluated once at the last prompt activation and reused for the continuation. For \Cobras it replaces $g_q$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:step}. For another method that maps an activation $\boldsymbol{h}$ to $\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}$, the gated output is $\boldsymbol{h}+g_{\mathrm{kNN}}\,(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}-\boldsymbol{h})$, so the gate scales each method's own displacement. \textcolor{blue}{Algorithm~\ref{alg:knn-external} summarizes how the gate is attached to another steering method.}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textcolor{blue}{Applying the kNN gate to a steering method}}
\label{alg:knn-external}
\small
\begingroup
\color{blue}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require A fitted steering method, projected undesired activations $\mathcal{D}^-\subset\Sph$, sphere radius $R$, layer $\ell$, and gate parameters $k,\tau,\gamma$
\Statex \textbf{Offline calibration}
\State Compute $\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}$ from leave-one-out neighbor distances using Eq.~\eqref{eq:knn-gate}
\Statex \textbf{For each new prompt}
\State Obtain the last prompt activation $\boldsymbol{h}$ at layer $\ell$ and project it to $\boldsymbol{x}=R\boldsymbol{h}/\|\boldsymbol{h}\|$
\State Compute and cache $g_q\gets g_{\mathrm{kNN}}(\boldsymbol{x})$ using Eq.~\eqref{eq:knn-gate}
\For{each token activation $\boldsymbol{h}$ at layer $\ell$, starting with the last prompt token}
    \State Compute the method's steering direction and apply its update to obtain $\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}$
    \State Replace $\boldsymbol{h}$ by $\boldsymbol{h}+g_q(\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}-\boldsymbol{h})$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\endgroup
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Connection to the product score.}
\label{apx:knn-connection}
\textcolor{blue}{The kernel factors connect the product score to nearest-neighbor support at small bandwidth.} For the undesired factor $\widehat{\varphi}_{b_g}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:frozen-factors}, write $\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)=\min_j d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_j^-)$. Every kernel in $\widehat{\varphi}_{b_g}$ is at most $\exp[-\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)^2/(2b_g)]$, with equality at a nearest sample. Bounding the weight of that sample below by $\min_j a_j^-$ and the total weight above by $\sum_j a_j^-$ gives
\begin{equation}
0\le -2b_g\log\frac{\widehat{\varphi}_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\sum_j a_j^-}
-\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)^2
\le 2b_g\log\frac{\sum_j a_j^-}{\min_j a_j^-}.
\label{eq:knn-kernel-bound}
\end{equation}
The scalings are fitted with $\varepsilon$ fixed and do not depend on $b_g$, so the upper bound vanishes as $b_g\to0$. The same argument applies to $\widehat{\psi}_{b_g}$ with $\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^+)=\min_i d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{h}_i^+)$, and summing the two gives
\begin{equation}
-2b_g\log\frac{\hat{p}_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})}
{(\sum_j a_j^-)(\sum_i a_i^+)}
\longrightarrow
\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^-)^2
+\rho_1(\boldsymbol{x};\mathcal{D}^+)^2
\quad\text{as }b_g\to0.
\label{eq:knn-product-limit}
\end{equation}
\textcolor{blue}{The left-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:knn-product-limit} is $2b_g u(\boldsymbol{x})$ plus a query-independent constant, so it preserves the ranks used in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gate}. The score $u$ therefore combines proximity to both populations. The kNN gate uses the proximity information captured by the undesired factor in Eq.~\eqref{eq:knn-kernel-bound}, replacing the nearest sample with the $k$-th neighbor to reduce dependence on an individual activation. Thus kNN serves as a distance-based surrogate for support control, while the product score retains both populations and their fitted transport scalings.}

\subsection{Calibration and sensitivity of the nearest-neighbor gate}
\label{apx:knn-calibration}
The distance gate is sensitive to its quantile because its reference population and its queries come from different token positions. In Eq.~\eqref{eq:knn-gate}, $\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is computed from contrastive question--answer activations, whereas the gate is evaluated at the last prompt token. In a Llama3.1-8B diagnostic, the median TruthfulQA prompt lies at the $96$th percentile of the reference distances and the median GSM8K prompt at the $99.2$nd, which leaves a range of only a few percentiles in which $\tau$ separates the two.

Table~\ref{tab:abstain-results} shows the resulting sensitivity. Raising $\tau$ enlarges the reference radius and permits stronger steering, which improves TruthfulQA True$\times$Info but sharply reduces GSM8K accuracy on Llama3.1-8B at $\tau=0.995$. Raising $k$ has a similar effect on Llama3.1-8B and little effect on Mistral-7B. Calibrating on prompt activations at the evaluated position removes this mismatch, which is how the product-score gate is calibrated (Section~\ref{subsec:strength}). On the same $100$ GSM8K questions, sweeping the distance gate over $\tau\in\{0.90,0.95,0.98,0.995\}$ gives $57$, $57$, $52$, and $15\%$ accuracy, whereas sweeping the product-score coverage over $p\in\{0.4,0.7,0.8,0.9\}$ gives $56$, $56$, $54$, and $52\%$. Appendix~\ref{apx:ood-gate} evaluates the product-score gate on the full OOD benchmarks.

\subsection{Base LLM Models}

Our base LLMs follow the experimental setup of Zhao~\etal~\cite{zhaoodesteer}. We describe them here for completeness. We evaluate all methods on four open-source models: (i) Falcon-7B, \texttt{tiiuae/falcon-7b}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/tiiuae/falcon-7b}}; (ii) Mistral-7B-v0.3, \texttt{mistralai/Mistral-7B-v0.3}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/mistralai/Mistral-7B-v0.3}}; (iii) LLaMA3.1-8B, \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B}}; and (iv) Qwen2.5-7B, \texttt{Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B}}. We use the same generation configuration for all models and experiments: temperature $=0.7$, top-$p=0.9$, and repetition penalty $=1.1$.


\subsection{Datasets}


\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TruthfulQA}~\citep{lin2021truthfulqa} is a benchmark consisting of 817 questions paired with true and false reference answers, along with source information. The questions cover 38 diverse categories and are commonly evaluated in a zero-shot setting. Following Zhao~\etal~\cite{zhaoodesteer}, we construct 5,918 question--answer pairs and use a 40/10/50 train/validation/test split with two-fold cross validation.

    \item \textbf{UltraFeedback}~\citep{cui2023ultrafeedback} is a large-scale alignment dataset covering instruction-following qualities such as truthfulness, honesty, and helpfulness. In our experiments, we use the UltraFeedback-Binarized dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceH4/ultrafeedback_binarized}}. Specifically, we use the preference-modeling split, where each prompt is paired with an accepted response and a rejected response. Following Zhao~\etal~\cite{zhaoodesteer}, we randomly sample 10K training pairs, 500 validation pairs, and 500 test pairs.

    \item \textbf{RealToxicityPrompts}~\citep{gehman2020realtoxicityprompts} is a benchmark for evaluating toxic degeneration in language models. It consists of naturally occurring web prompts paired with toxicity annotations obtained from the Perspective API. The dataset contains approximately 21K toxic prompts with toxicity scores greater than 0.5 and 77K non-toxic prompts. Following Zhao~\etal~\citep{zhaoodesteer}, we train toxicity steering methods using the Jigsaw Unintended Bias in Toxicity Classification dataset\footnote{\url{https://www.kaggle.com/c/jigsaw-unintended-bias-in-toxicity-classification}} and evaluate them on RealToxicityPrompts. Specifically, we construct a balanced Jigsaw training set of 10K sentences by sampling according to toxicity scores, with 5K toxic and 5K benign examples. For evaluation, we select 500 toxic prompts from RealToxicityPrompts and use them as inputs to the LLMs. This setup allows us to assess whether steering methods reduce toxic generations while preserving response quality.

    \item \textbf{TriviaQA}~\citep{joshi2017triviaqa} is a large-scale dataset consisting over 650 triplets of trivia questions - answers - evidences. The questions are naturally occurring and often require lexical, syntactic, and multi-sentence reasoning. In our experiments, we use the a subset of the original dataset, specifcially, the huggingface dataset which is tailored for Inference Time Intervenetion: ~\href{https://huggingface.co/datasets/OamPatel/iti_trivia_qa_val}{TriviaQA HF Dataset}. 

    \item \textbf{Natural Questions (NQ)}~\citep{kwiatkowski2019natural} is a question-answering dataset constructed from real, anonymized Google search queries. In our experiments, we use the modified NQ-Open validation set released by the ITI authors for transfer evaluation: \href{https://huggingface.co/datasets/OamPatel/iti_nq_open_val}{NQ HF Dataset}. All steering methods are trained on TruthfulQA, as described in the OOD evaluation section, and evaluated on a randomly sampled subset of 1K NQ examples.

    \item \textbf{GSM8K}~\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k} contains approximately 8.5K grade-school math word problems paired with natural-language solutions. The dataset contains roughly 7.5K training examples and 1K test examples. We use the original test split for out-of-distribution evaluation. All methods are trained on TruthfulQA as described above and then evaluated on the 1K GSM8K test examples in a 5-shot setting.

    \item \textbf{MMLU}~\citep{hendrycks2020measuring} is a large-scale multiple-choice benchmark covering a broad range of academic and professional subjects. It contains approximately 100K training examples and 14K test examples. We use the default test split for out-of-distribution evaluation and perform 5-shot generation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{appendix:metrics}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Truthfulness (\%).}
    Truthfulness is evaluated on TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2021truthfulqa} using a publicly available fine-tuned LLaMA2-7B judge\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/allenai/truthfulqa-truth-judge-llama2-7B}}. The judge predicts a binary truthfulness label for each generated answer using prompts of the form:
    \[
    \texttt{Q: \{question\} \textbackslash n A: \{answer\}. \textbackslash n True:}
    \]
    We report the percentage of generations classified as truthful.

    \item \textbf{Informativeness (\%).}
    Informativeness is evaluated on TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2021truthfulqa} using a publicly available fine-tuned LLaMA2-7B judge\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/allenai/truthfulqa-info-judge-llama2-7B}}. The judge predicts whether a response is informative or helpful using prompts of the form:
    \[
    \texttt{Q: \{question\} \textbackslash n A: \{answer\}. \textbackslash n Helpful:}
    \]
    We report the percentage of generations classified as informative.

    \item \textbf{Truthfulness $\times$ Informativeness (\%).}
    Following prior work, we report the percentage of responses that are both truthful and informative. For each prompt, this metric is computed as the product of the binary truthfulness and informativeness labels, and the final score is averaged over all prompts.

    \item \textbf{Win Rate (\%).}
    We adopt the reward-model win-rate metric used in RewardBench~\citep{lambert2025rewardbench} and ODESteer~\citep{zhaoodesteer}. Given a set of prompts $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$ and two candidate systems $A$ and $B$, let $s_i^A$ and $s_i^B$ denote their reward-model scores under the same reward model. The win rate of $A$ over $B$ is defined as
    \[
    \mathrm{Win}(A,B)
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \left[
    \mathbbm{1}(s_i^A > s_i^B)
    +
    \frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}(s_i^A = s_i^B)
    \right],
    \]
    where $\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function. A value of $0.5$ indicates parity with system $B$, values greater than $0.5$ indicate that $A$ outperforms $B$, and ties contribute $0.5$ by convention.

    \item \textbf{RM$_{\text{mean}}$ and RM$_{90}$.}
    For UltraFeedback, we use an LLM-as-a-judge reward model to score helpfulness. Specifically, we use \texttt{Skywork/Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/Skywork/Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B}}. We report RM$_{\text{mean}}$, the average reward score across examples and three random seeds, and RM$_{90}$, the 90th percentile reward score.

    \item \textbf{Toxicity Score.}
    Toxicity is evaluated using the Perspective API\footnote{\url{https://perspectiveapi.com/}}. The toxicity score is a probability-like score indicating the likelihood that a reader would perceive the generated text as toxic. Lower values therefore indicate less toxic generations.

    \item \textbf{Perplexity.}
    We evaluate generation fluency using perplexity under GPT-2-XL~\cite{radford2019language}. Given a generated response $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_T)$, GPT-2-XL assigns a next-token negative log-likelihood
    \[
    \mathcal{L}(y)
    =
    -\frac{1}{T-1}
    \sum_{t=1}^{T-1}
    \log p_{\text{GPT2-XL}}(y_{t+1}\mid y_{\leq t}).
    \]
    The perplexity of $y$ is then,
    $\mathrm{PPL}(y)
    =
    \exp\left(\mathcal{L}(y)\right).$ Lower perplexity indicates that the generated text is more fluent under GPT-2-XL.

    \item \textbf{Dist-$n$.}
    We measure lexical diversity using Dist-$n$ for $n\in\{1,2,3\}$. For a generated response $y$, let $\mathcal{G}_n(y)$ denote the multiset of all word-level $n$-grams in $y$, obtained by whitespace tokenization. Dist-$n$ is defined as
    \[
    \mathrm{Dist}\text{-}n(y)
    =
    \frac{
    \left|\mathrm{Unique}(\mathcal{G}_n(y))\right|
    }{
    \left|\mathcal{G}_n(y)\right|
    }.
    \]
    If the response contains fewer than $n$ words, we set $\mathrm{Dist}\text{-}n(y)=0$. Higher Dist-$n$ indicates greater lexical diversity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implementation Details}


\label{appendix:implementation_details}

All experiments use one H100 80GB GPU. We extend the ODESteer codebase\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ZhaoHongjue/odesteer}} to implement the steering methods and baselines. We set $\varepsilon=\sigma^2$ from geodesic neighborhood distances in $\mathcal{D}^+\cup\mathcal{D}^-$. The steering bandwidths follow Eq.~\eqref{eq:adaptive-bandwidth}, and we use $K=10$ integration steps. The accompanying code specifies the bandwidth settings, Sinkhorn initialization and iteration count, concentration $\kappa$, and numerical safeguards. The in-distribution configuration is ungated, with $g_q=1$ and $T=0.5$. The OOD configuration uses $p=0.7$ and $T=0.65$. Steering acts through a forward hook at layer $\ell$ on the last-token activation at every decoding step. Our ITI baseline uses the normalized logistic-probe direction implemented in this codebase.

\subsection{Empirical Validation of Spherical Geometry assumption}
\label{appendix:norm_concentration}

\Cobras models residual-stream activations at a fixed layer as lying approximately on a hypersphere. \subsection{Diversity on Detoxification}


Table~\ref{tab:detox_dist} shows the generation diversity of steering methods on the detoxification task using Dist-$n$ metrics. Overall, \Cobras preserves lexical diversity close to the original model across all four backbones. The gap is small for Mistral-7B and LLaMA3.1-8B, where Dist-2 and Dist-3 remain nearly unchanged compared to the original model.

We also observe that some baselines reduce diversity more noticeably. For example, CAA lowers diversity on Falcon-7B and Qwen2.5-7B, while SphericalSteer shows a larger drop on Qwen2.5-7B. In contrast, \Cobras maintains high Dist-2 and Dist-3 scores, suggesting that its detoxification effect does not come from generating overly repetitive or generic responses.



\subsection{Impact of geodesic Euler steps $K$}

Table~\ref{tab:cobras_truthfulqa_iters} shows the effect of increasing the number of geodesic Euler steps $K$ in our proposed \Cobras steering method on TruthfulQA. Overall, increasing $K$ improves the True $\times$ Info score across all models, although the trend differs slightly by model.

For Falcon-7B, larger $K$ increases informativeness, but also reduces the True score, leading to only a modest gain in True $\times$ Info. For Mistral-7B, performance is relatively stable across different values of $K$, with a small improvement at $K=10$. LLaMA3.1-8B benefits the most from increasing $K$, where True $\times$ Info improves consistently from $64.6$ at $K=1$ to $68.5$ at $K=10$. These results suggest that using more geodesic Euler steps generally improves steering quality, with $K=10$ giving the best overall performance in our experiments.

\subsection{Performance vs Steering Strength for baselines}

To select the steering strength for each baseline, we sweep over model-specific ranges: $T=20$--$23$ for Falcon-7B, $T=2$--$5$ for Mistral-7B, $T=3$--$6$ for LLaMA3.1-8B, and $T=13$--$16$ for Qwen2.5-7B. Figure~\ref{fig:performance_vs_t} shows the performance of RepE, ITI, CAA, and ODESteer on TruthfulQA and UltraFeedback. For baselines without a comparable steering-strength parameter, we use the best values reported in their papers.

The results show that the best steering strength depends on both the model and the method. In general, some methods improve TruthfulQA performance at moderate values of $T$, but larger steering strengths can hurt UltraFeedback performance. This trend is especially visible for ODESteer on Mistral-7B and LLaMA3.1-8B, where increasing $T$ leads to a large drop in UltraFeedback win rate. CAA is more stable on UltraFeedback and often improves with larger $T$, while ITI tends to change more gradually across the sweep.
