\section{A Unified Log-density Ratio Optimization Framework for Activation Steering}
\label{apx:ldr-formalize}

In this section, we show that diverse activation steering methodologies can be unified under a single theoretical framework governed by log-density ratio optimization. Specifically, we analyze how these seemingly disparate techniques implicitly optimize the ratio $\log(p_+ / p_-)$ by reinterpreting them as specific geometric configurations of a continuous global flow. Formally, let $p_{+}$ and $p_{-}$ define a pair of continuous densities corresponding to the contrastive behavior distributions $\mathcal{D}^{+}$ and $\mathcal{D}^{-}$, respectively. Given an initial query activation $\boldsymbol{h}_q$ on an underlying representation manifold $\mathcal{M}$, the steering trajectory is obtained by integrating the continuous-time ordinary differential equation:

\begin{equation}
d\boldsymbol{x}_t = \nabla_{\mathcal{M}}\log\frac{p_{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})}{p_{-}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})}\,dt, \qquad \boldsymbol{x}_{0} = \hquery,
\label{eq:global-flow}
\end{equation}

for $K$ steps over a specified time horizon $[0, T]$. Here, $\boldsymbol{x}_{t}$ denotes the steered activation along the flow, initialized as $\boldsymbol{x}_{0} = \hquery$. By systematically varying the manifold geometry $\mathcal{M}$, the step horizon $K$, and the parameterization of the density functions, we can recover prominent steering paradigms from this single formulation~\footnote{\citet{zhaoodesteer} presents a related unification of several of these methods, but their analysis is confined to Euclidean space; by treating the manifold $\mathcal{M}$ as an explicit design choice, however, our framework additionally captures geometry-aware methods such as SS and HPR.}.

For example, the following methods can be recalled from this perspective as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:steering_framework}.




\paragraph{Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA)~\citep{rimsky2024steering}} The  CAA algorithm can be framed as a specific, single-step optimization instantiation of a continuous global flow over the hidden state manifold. Specifically,  CAA simplifies this trajectory by optimizing over a flat Euclidean space $\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^d$ via a single discrete step ($K=1$). By modeling the factual (positive) and counterfactual (negative) activation distributions as isotropic Gaussians sharing an identity covariance $p_{+}=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}, \boldsymbol{I}_d)$ and $p_{-}=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{-}, \boldsymbol{I}_d)$, the complex continuous gradient field collapses into a constant shift vector defined by the difference of their respective means  $\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}\log\frac{p_{+}}{p_{-}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_+ - \boldsymbol{\mu}_-$. Consequently, the empirical practice of injecting a static difference vector during the forward pass is mathematically grounded as an exact, first-order approximation toward maximizing the target likelihood ratio.

\paragraph{Spherical Steering (SS)~\citep{you2026spherical}}


To relax the assumption of a flat Euclidean space and account for the directional nature of high-dimensional neural representations, Spherical Steering constrains the optimization trajectory to a unit hypersphere manifold, setting $\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{S}^{d-1}$. Under a single-step discrete horizon ($K=1$), the method models the target positive activations using a von Mises-Fisher (vMF) distribution $p_+=\mathrm{vMF}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mu},\kappa)$ which acts as the spherical analogue to an isotropic Gaussian with a mean direction vector $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and a concentration parameter $\kappa$. Conversely, the negative behavior is modeled as a uniform distribution over the sphere $p_-=\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{S})$. The unit prototype towards the positive activations $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is empirically defined by normalizing the difference between the mean positive and negative activations:
$$\boldsymbol{\mu}= \frac{\bar{\boldsymbol{h}}^{+} - \bar{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}}{\|\bar{\boldsymbol{h}}^{+} - \bar{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}\|}.$$

When evaluating the global flow framework under these spherical constraints, the Riemannian gradient of the likelihood ratio yields a non-linear direction proportional to the logarithmic map on the sphere 
$\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\log\tfrac{p_+}{p_-}
\propto\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})$ which corresponds to the Slerp direction toward $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ in
the direction of this Riemannian gradient. By explicitly respecting the spherical geometry of hidden states, SS ensures that the magnitude of the activation vector is preserved, avoiding out-of-distribution distortion while performing directional steering.

\paragraph{Inference-Time Intervention (ITI) \citep{li2023inference}} Rather than assuming explicit parametric distributions for the hidden states, ITI leverages a discriminative classifier to implicitly estimate the underlying density ratio. Operating within a single-step horizon ($K=1$) over a flat Euclidean vector space $\mathcal{M} = \mathbb{R}^d$, ITI trains a linear logistic probe, $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \boldsymbol{x})$, to distinguish between positive and negative activation vectors. By invoking the classic equivalence between logistic regression and density ratio estimation, the log-density ratio can be rewritten linearly as $\log \frac{p_+(\boldsymbol{x})}{p_-(\boldsymbol{x})} = \boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \boldsymbol{x} + \log \frac{N_-}{N_+}$, where $N_+$ and $N_-$ represent the respective sample counts. Taking the gradient with respect to the manifold yields a constant vector field defined entirely by the probe's learned weights:$$\nabla_{\mathcal{M}} \log \frac{p_+}{p_-} = \boldsymbol{\theta}$$This formulation reveals a powerful geometric justification for ITI: the orthogonal vector to the classifier's decision boundary acts as the exact gradient direction needed to maximize the factual likelihood ratio. Consequently, shifting activations along the direction of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ during inference provides a theoretically grounded, first-order trajectory for target behavior optimization.
\paragraph{Householder Reflection Probe (HPR)~\citep{pham2024householder}}  To reconcile discriminative, probe-based steering with non-Euclidean representation spaces, the HPR method maps the log-density ratio directly onto a hyperspherical manifold ($\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{S}^{d-1}$) under a single-step horizon ($K=1$). Similar to ITI, the method first projects the log-density ratio linearly using a trained linear probe, $\log \frac{p_+(\boldsymbol{x})}{p_-(\boldsymbol{x})} = \boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \boldsymbol{x} + \log \frac{N_-}{N_+}$. To respect the spherical constraints, HPR constructs an orthogonal Householder reflection matrix, $\boldsymbol{H} = \boldsymbol{I} - 2\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u}^\top$, where $\boldsymbol{u} = \boldsymbol{\theta} / \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|$ represents the unit normal vector to the probe's decision boundary. By reflecting the normalized activation vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}} = \boldsymbol{x} / \|\boldsymbol{x}\|$ across this boundary, the directional gradient on the sphere is formalized as $\nabla_{\mathcal{S}} \log \frac{p_+(\boldsymbol{x})}{p_-(\boldsymbol{x})} \propto \Pi_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}(\boldsymbol{H}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}} - \hat{\boldsymbol{x}})$, where $\Pi_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}$ denotes the projection onto the tangent space at $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}$. Geometrically, this confirms that the optimal trajectory corresponds to a Spherical Linear Interpolation (Slerp) toward the reflected state $\boldsymbol{H}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}$. By leveraging a reflection operator, HPR effectively utilizes the probe's decision boundaries to execute directional steering while intrinsically preserving the latent norm of the activation path.

\paragraph{ODESteer~\citep{zhaoodesteer}} Departing from single-step first-order approximations ($K=1$), ODESteer fully embraces the continuous-time formulation of the global flow framework by integrating across multiple steps ($K > 1$) over a flat Euclidean vector space $\mathcal{M} = \mathbb{R}^d$. Rather than adopting simple parametric distributions or linear probes, this approach models complex, non-linear activation dynamics by parameterizing the log-density ratio with a polynomial count-sketch feature map $\phi$ and setting $\log \frac{p_+(\boldsymbol{x})}{p_-(\boldsymbol{x})} = \boldsymbol{w}^\top \phi(\boldsymbol{x}) + b$, where the parameter pair $(\boldsymbol{w}, b)$ is fit empirically over the contrastive dataset. Under this non-linear projection, the steering direction is dictated by a dynamic, normalized vector field:$$\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \frac{\boldsymbol{J}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})^\top \boldsymbol{w}}{\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})^\top \boldsymbol{w}\|}$$where $\boldsymbol{J}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})$ denotes the Jacobian matrix of the feature map $\phi$ evaluated at the current state $\boldsymbol{x}$. By evaluating and integrating this state-dependent vector field using discrete Euler steps over the time horizon $[0, T]$, ODESteer traces a curved, non-linear path through the representation space. This multi-step integration allows the model to continuously adapt to the changing manifold geometry during the steering process, providing precise control while tracking complex latent boundaries.

\section{Derivations and Implementation}
\label{apx:theory}

This appendix derives the continuous bridge field, explains the discrete transport fit,
and verifies the finite-sample direction and its density-ratio limit. All geodesic gradients
are taken away from sample antipodes, where squared geodesic distance is not differentiable.
The gauge change $(\boldsymbol a^-,\boldsymbol a^+)\mapsto
(\lambda\boldsymbol a^-,\lambda^{-1}\boldsymbol a^+)$ leaves the coupling, steering
weights, and support product unchanged.

\subsection{Continuous bridge and discrete transport}
\label{apx:static}

\paragraph{Probability flow.}
The continuous bridge has drift
$\boldsymbol u(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\sigma^2\nablaS\log\psi(\boldsymbol{x},t)$
and diffusion generator $\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\Delta_{\Sph}$.
Its Fokker--Planck equation can be written
\begin{align}
\partial_t p_t
&=-\operatorname{div}_{\Sph}(p_t\boldsymbol u)
  +\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Delta_{\Sph}p_t \nonumber\\
&=-\operatorname{div}_{\Sph}\!\left[
p_t\left(\boldsymbol u-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\nablaS\log p_t\right)\right].
\label{eq:apx-continuity}
\end{align}
This is the continuity equation for a deterministic field with the same marginals,
initialized from $\pminus$. Substituting $p_t\propto\varphi\psi$ gives
$\boldsymbol u-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\nablaS\log p_t
=\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\nablaS\log(\psi/\varphi)$, proving Eq.~\eqref{eq:ldr}.

\paragraph{Static formulation.}
Disintegrating a path law into its endpoint coupling $P_{01}$ and its conditional
path laws $P_{\mid01}$ gives~\citep{leonard2013survey}
\begin{equation}
\KL(P\,\Vert\,Q)
=\KL(P_{01}\,\Vert\,Q_{01})
+\mathbb E_{P_{01}}\!\left[\KL(P_{\mid01}\,\Vert\,Q_{\mid01})\right].
\label{eq:apx-disint}
\end{equation}
For any endpoint coupling, the second term is minimized by retaining the reference
bridges. Thus the continuous problem reduces to entropy minimization over endpoint couplings.
Atomic empirical endpoints would have infinite relative entropy against Brownian endpoints;
we therefore discretize the static problem rather than impose empirical measures directly
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sb}.

Choose discrete reference weights proportional to $e^{-c_{ji}}$ and normalize them
to sum to one. Their relative entropy objective differs by a constant from
\begin{equation}
\pi^\star=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\pi\in\mathcal U}
\sum_{j,i}\pi_{ji}c_{ji}+\sum_{j,i}\pi_{ji}\log\pi_{ji},
\quad
\mathcal U=\left\{\pi\ge0:
\sum_i\pi_{ji}=\frac1{N_-},\
\sum_j\pi_{ji}=\frac1{N_+}\right\}.
\label{eq:e-ot}
\end{equation}
The strictly positive kernel gives a unique optimal coupling with the factorization
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:plan}. Sinkhorn fitting alternates the log-space updates
\begin{align}
\log a_j^-&=-\log N_- -
\operatorname{logsumexp}_i[\log a_i^+-c_{ji}],\nonumber\\
\log a_i^+&=-\log N_+ -
\operatorname{logsumexp}_j[\log a_j^--c_{ji}].
\label{eq:sinkhorn}
\end{align}
At convergence the plan satisfies both marginals. Five sweeps, as used in the
implementation, give an approximate fit. The choice of geodesic affinity and subsequent
stationary field do not preserve the exact Brownian bridge construction.

\subsection{Kernel gradients and conditional weights}
\label{app:grad-psi}

For $\theta=d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h)/R<\pi$, differentiating
$R^2\arccos^2(R^{-2}\boldsymbol{x}^\top\boldsymbol h)$ and projecting onto the tangent space gives
\[
\nablaS d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h)^2
=-\frac{2\theta}{\sin\theta}\Pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol h
=-2\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol h).
\]
At $\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol h$, use the continuous limit, which is zero.
For fixed positive $m_i$, the chain rule therefore yields
\begin{equation}
b\nablaS\log\sum_i m_iK_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_i)
=\sum_i w_i(\boldsymbol{x})\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol h_i),
\quad
w_i(\boldsymbol{x})=
\frac{m_iK_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_i)}
{\sum_{i'}m_{i'}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_{i'})}.
\label{eq:grad-psi}
\end{equation}
Taking $m_i=a_i^+$ or $m_j=a_j^-$ gives the log-gradients of the fitted kernel
factors. Uniform $m_i$ gives the corresponding kernel density-estimate gradient.

\paragraph{What is held fixed in the conditional field.}
\label{apx:omitted-term}
At a steering iterate $\boldsymbol q\in\Sph$, define the local objective
\begin{equation}
F_{\boldsymbol q}(\boldsymbol y)=
\log\frac{\sum_i m^+_{i,b}(\boldsymbol q)K_b(\boldsymbol y,\boldsymbol h_i^+)}
{\sum_j m^-_{j,b}(\boldsymbol q)K_b(\boldsymbol y,\boldsymbol h_j^-)}.
\label{eq:local-objective}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:grad-psi} gives
$\boldsymbol v(\boldsymbol q)=b\nabla_{\boldsymbol y}F_{\boldsymbol q}(\boldsymbol y)
|_{\boldsymbol y=\boldsymbol q}$. The coefficients are fixed during this differentiation
and recomputed at the next iterate. This is an exact local interpretation.

In contrast, let $\Psi^\pm_b(\boldsymbol{x})$ use the same activation in both the
multipliers and the kernels. Differentiating these composites gives
\begin{equation}
b\nablaS\log\frac{\Psi_b^+}{\Psi_b^-}
=\boldsymbol v
+b\sum_i\bar w_i^+\nablaS\log m^+_{i,b}
-b\sum_j\bar w_j^-\nablaS\log m^-_{j,b},
\label{eq:apx-omitted}
\end{equation}
with every term evaluated at $\boldsymbol{x}$. The extra terms need not vanish.
We therefore make no claim that the conditional field follows a single global
potential ratio or exactly solves Eq.~\eqref{eq:sb}.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:ldr-limit}}
\label{apx:proof-ldr-limit}

\begin{proof}
Write $S_-(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_j a_j^-K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_j^-)$.
Since $0\le c_{ji}\le c_{\max}$, every desired multiplier satisfies
\[
e^{-c_{\max}}S_-(\boldsymbol{x})
\le m^+_{i,b}(\boldsymbol{x})\le S_-(\boldsymbol{x}).
\]
Its log-range across $i$ is therefore at most $c_{\max}$, independently of the
positive scalings. The undesired multipliers obey the same bound.

For a probability vector $u$, reweighting by positive multipliers of log-range
at most $c_{\max}$ gives $w_i=u_i m_i/\sum_k u_km_k$. After a common rescaling,
both $m_i$ and their $u$-weighted mean lie in $[1,e^{c_{\max}}]$. Consequently,
$\|w-u\|_1\le e^{c_{\max}}-1$.
Apply this to the kernel-only weights
$u_i^+\propto K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_i^+)$ and the conditional weights
$\bar w_i^+$. Each log-map has norm at most $\pi R$, so
\[
\left\|\sum_i(\bar w_i^+-u_i^+)
\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol h_i^+)\right\|
\le\pi R(e^{c_{\max}}-1).
\]
The negative branch contributes the same bound. Equation~\eqref{eq:grad-psi}
identifies their kernel-only difference with $\boldsymbol v_b^{\mathrm{kde}}$,
proving Eq.~\eqref{eq:prop1-bound}.
Finally, $c_{\max}\le\pi^2R^2/(2\varepsilon)$ gives uniform convergence and the
$O(1/\varepsilon)$ rate at fixed $b$.
\end{proof}

The bound concerns unnormalized fields. Normalized directions also converge
where the limiting field's norm is bounded away from zero, but this need not
hold near stationary points. The proposition is a transport-scale limit
on a fixed dataset, not a statistical consistency result.

\subsection{Adaptive bandwidths and pseudocode}
\label{apx:bandwidth}
\label{apx:pseudocode}

The implementation selects a squared-distance scale separately for each
population at every iterate:
\begin{equation}
b_\pm(\boldsymbol{x})=
\max\!\left\{\max_{\boldsymbol h\in\mathcal D^\pm}
d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h)^2,\ (10^{-3}R)^2\right\}.
\label{eq:adaptive-bandwidth}
\end{equation}
Every kernel centered on a desired sample uses $b_+$, including the kernels
inside $m^-$. Every kernel centered on an undesired sample uses $b_-$,
including those inside $m^+$. The final field remains the difference of
weighted log-maps in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cobras-field}; neither branch is divided by
its bandwidth. Thus the common-$b$ gradient-ratio identity and
Proposition~\ref{prop:ldr-limit} describe the fixed-bandwidth construction.
The gate uses the separate, fixed bandwidth $b_g$.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:cobras} gives the adaptive procedure. Kernel sums and
normalized weights are evaluated in log space. Implementations also clamp
cosines away from $\pm1$ and vector norms away from zero for numerical stability.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\Cobras with adaptive bandwidths}
\label{alg:cobras}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\mathcal D^\pm$; reference prompts $\mathcal D_{\mathrm{ref}}$;
strength $T$; coverage $p$; steps $K$; Sinkhorn sweeps $n_{\mathrm{S}}$; concentration $\kappa$
\Statex \textbf{Offline: fit transport and calibrate support}
\State $R\gets\mathrm{mean}_{\boldsymbol h\in\mathcal D^+\cup\mathcal D^-}\|\boldsymbol h\|$;
project all contrastive activations onto $\Sph$
\State $\varepsilon\gets\max\{(\text{median distance to the fifth nearest neighbor})^2,(10^{-3}R)^2\}$
\State Form $c_{ji}$ using Eq.~\eqref{eq:plan}; fit $\log\boldsymbol a^\pm$
with $n_{\mathrm S}$ sweeps of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sinkhorn}, starting from $\log\boldsymbol a^+=0$
\State Compute $\boldsymbol\mu$ using Eq.~\eqref{eq:mu}; set $b_g\gets\varepsilon/256$
\State Store the sorted scores $u(R\boldsymbol z/\|\boldsymbol z\|)$
for $\boldsymbol z\in\mathcal D_{\mathrm{ref}}$
\Statex \textbf{Online: edit the current token activation $\hquery$}
\State $\boldsymbol{x}_0\gets R\hquery/\|\hquery\|$
\label{alg:cobras:norm_equation}
\If{this is the last prompt token}
    \State Compute $g_q$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:gate} and cache it for the continuation
\EndIf
\State $s\gets s(\boldsymbol{x}_0)$;\quad
$\Delta\gets\tfrac{\pi}{2}Tsg_qR/K$
\If{$\Delta=0$}
    \State \Return $\hquery$
\EndIf
\For{$k=0,\ldots,K-1$}
    \State Compute $b_\pm(\boldsymbol{x}_k)$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:adaptive-bandwidth}
    \State $L_i^+\gets-d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x}_k,\boldsymbol h_i^+)^2/(2b_+)$;
    $L_j^-\gets-d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x}_k,\boldsymbol h_j^-)^2/(2b_-)$
    \State $M_i^+\gets\operatorname{logsumexp}_j[\log a_j^-+L_j^--c_{ji}]$;
    $M_j^-\gets\operatorname{logsumexp}_i[\log a_i^++L_i^+-c_{ji}]$
    \State $\bar w_i^+\gets\mathrm{softmax}_i(M_i^++L_i^+)$;
    $\bar w_j^-\gets\mathrm{softmax}_j(M_j^-+L_j^-)$
    \State Form $\boldsymbol v_k$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:cobras-field}
    \If{$\|\boldsymbol v_k\|>0$}
        \State $\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}\gets
        \exp_{\boldsymbol{x}_k}(\Delta\,\boldsymbol v_k/\|\boldsymbol v_k\|)$
    \Else
        \State $\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}\gets\boldsymbol{x}_k$
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\|\hquery\|\boldsymbol{x}_K/R$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Calibration and Properties of the Support Gate}
\label{apx:ood-gate}

\paragraph{Reference prompts.}
The gate uses the product score in Eq.~\eqref{eq:support-score}, with
$b_g=\varepsilon/256$. Both reference and query activations are taken at the
steering layer and last prompt position, projected onto $\Sph$, and scored
identically. This avoids assuming that prompt-only and contrastive
question--answer activations have the same score distribution. A difference
in raw activation norms alone cannot explain a score shift after projection.

In the TruthfulQA gate analysis, $\mathcal D_{\mathrm{ref}}$ contains the
training-fold prompts and the gate is applied to the held-out fold.
For OOD evaluation, it contains all $817$ TruthfulQA prompts. No GSM8K,
MMLU, NQ, or TriviaQA prompt is used for calibration.

\paragraph{Finite-reference coverage.}
\begin{lemma}[Empirical-rank calibration]
\label{lem:coverage}
Let $n$ reference scores and one new score be exchangeable and almost surely
distinct. For the empirical rank $r$ and gate in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gate},
\begin{equation}
\Pr(g=1)=\frac{\lfloor np\rfloor+1}{n+1},
\qquad
\mathbb E[g]=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{j=0}^{n}
\min\!\left\{1,\frac{1-j/n}{1-p}\right\}.
\label{eq:gate-finite-coverage}
\end{equation}
For fixed $p<1$, these converge to $p$ and $(1+p)/2$, respectively.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Exchangeability and absence of ties make the new score's position among the
$n+1$ scores uniform. Thus $nr$ is uniform on $\{0,\ldots,n\}$, giving both
formulas. The mean tends to
$\int_0^p1\,dr+(1-p)^{-1}\int_p^1(1-r)\,dr=(1+p)/2$.
\end{proof}

The expectation averages over both reference and query scores; it is not a
conditional guarantee for every realized reference set. A score function
fitted independently of i.i.d.\ reference and query prompts is a sufficient
condition. Reusing bridge-fitting prompts as references need not satisfy
that condition, and ties also alter the rank law.
Moreover, evaluating a reference set against itself gives the deterministic
ranks $0,1/n,\ldots,(n-1)/n$ when there are no ties. A mean gate near
$(1+p)/2$ on that set is therefore an arithmetic calibration property,
not evidence of coverage on unseen prompts or of OOD detection.

\paragraph{Small-bandwidth interpretation.}
At fixed samples and scalings, the score approaches a nearest-neighbor rule:
\begin{equation}
2b_g u(\boldsymbol{x})\longrightarrow
\min_j d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_j^-)^2+
\min_i d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_i^+)^2
\quad\text{as }b_g\to0.
\label{eq:gate-nn-limit}
\end{equation}
To see this, let $D_-=\min_j d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_j^-)^2$.
Then
$a^-_{\min}e^{-D_-/(2b_g)}
\le\widehat\varphi_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})
\le(\sum_j a_j^-)e^{-D_-/(2b_g)}$.
Taking $-2b_g\log$ bounds the error by constants times $b_g$, uniformly in
$\boldsymbol{x}$; the desired factor is identical. This explains the
score's proximity interpretation. It does not guarantee the ordering of
nearly tied prompts or separation of arbitrary OOD distributions.

\subsection{Base LLM Models}

Our base LLMs follow the experimental setup of Zhao~\etal~\cite{zhaoodesteer}. We describe them here for completeness. We evaluate all methods on four open-source models: (i) Falcon-7B, \texttt{tiiuae/falcon-7b}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/tiiuae/falcon-7b}}; (ii) Mistral-7B-v0.3, \texttt{mistralai/Mistral-7B-v0.3}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/mistralai/Mistral-7B-v0.3}}; (iii) LLaMA3.1-8B, \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B}}; and (iv) Qwen2.5-7B, \texttt{Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B}}. \subsection{Datasets}


\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TruthfulQA}~\citep{lin2021truthfulqa} is a benchmark consisting of 817 questions paired with true and false reference answers, along with source information. The questions cover 38 diverse categories and are commonly evaluated in a zero-shot setting. Following Zhao~\etal~\cite{zhaoodesteer}, we construct 5,918 question--answer pairs and use a 40/10/50 train/validation/test split with two-fold cross validation.

    \item \textbf{UltraFeedback}~\citep{cui2023ultrafeedback} is a large-scale alignment dataset covering instruction-following qualities such as truthfulness, honesty, and helpfulness. In our experiments, we use the UltraFeedback-Binarized dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceH4/ultrafeedback_binarized}}. Specifically, we use the preference-modeling split, where each prompt is paired with an accepted response and a rejected response. Following Zhao~\etal~\cite{zhaoodesteer}, we randomly sample 10K training pairs, 500 validation pairs, and 500 test pairs.

    \item \textbf{RealToxicityPrompts}~\citep{gehman2020realtoxicityprompts} is a benchmark for evaluating toxic degeneration in language models. It consists of naturally occurring web prompts paired with toxicity annotations obtained from the Perspective API. The dataset contains approximately 21K toxic prompts with toxicity scores greater than 0.5 and 77K non-toxic prompts. Following Zhao~\etal~\citep{zhaoodesteer}, we train toxicity steering methods using the Jigsaw Unintended Bias in Toxicity Classification dataset\footnote{\url{https://www.kaggle.com/c/jigsaw-unintended-bias-in-toxicity-classification}} and evaluate them on RealToxicityPrompts. Specifically, we construct a balanced Jigsaw training set of 10K sentences by sampling according to toxicity scores, with 5K toxic and 5K benign examples. For evaluation, we select 500 toxic prompts from RealToxicityPrompts and use them as inputs to the LLMs. This setup allows us to assess whether steering methods reduce toxic generations while preserving response quality.

    \item \textbf{TriviaQA}~\citep{joshi2017triviaqa} is a large-scale dataset consisting over 650 triplets of trivia questions - answers - evidences. The questions are naturally occurring and often require lexical, syntactic, and multi-sentence reasoning. In our experiments, we use the a subset of the original dataset, specifcially, the huggingface dataset which is tailored for Inference Time Intervenetion: ~\href{https://huggingface.co/datasets/OamPatel/iti_trivia_qa_val}{TriviaQA HF Dataset}. 

    \item \textbf{Natural Questions (NQ)}~\citep{kwiatkowski2019natural} is a question-answering dataset constructed from real, anonymized Google search queries. In our experiments, we use the modified NQ-Open validation set released by the ITI authors for transfer evaluation: \href{https://huggingface.co/datasets/OamPatel/iti_nq_open_val}{NQ HF Dataset}. All steering methods are trained on TruthfulQA, as described in the OOD evaluation section, and evaluated on a randomly sampled subset of 1K NQ examples.

    \item \textbf{GSM8K}~\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k} contains approximately 8.5K grade-school math word problems paired with natural-language solutions. The dataset contains roughly 7.5K training examples and 1K test examples. We use the original test split for out-of-distribution evaluation. All methods are trained on TruthfulQA as described above and then evaluated on the 1K GSM8K test examples in a 5-shot setting.

    \item \textbf{MMLU}~\citep{hendrycks2020measuring} is a large-scale multiple-choice benchmark covering a broad range of academic and professional subjects. It contains approximately 100K training examples and 14K test examples. We use the default test split for out-of-distribution evaluation and perform 5-shot generation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{appendix:metrics}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Truthfulness (\%).}
    Truthfulness is evaluated on TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2021truthfulqa} using a publicly available fine-tuned LLaMA2-7B judge\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/allenai/truthfulqa-truth-judge-llama2-7B}}. The judge predicts a binary truthfulness label for each generated answer using prompts of the form:
    \[
    \texttt{Q: \{question\} \textbackslash n A: \{answer\}. \textbackslash n True:}
    \]
    We report the percentage of generations classified as truthful.

    \item \textbf{Informativeness (\%).}
    Informativeness is evaluated on TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2021truthfulqa} using a publicly available fine-tuned LLaMA2-7B judge\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/allenai/truthfulqa-info-judge-llama2-7B}}. The judge predicts whether a response is informative or helpful using prompts of the form:
    \[
    \texttt{Q: \{question\} \textbackslash n A: \{answer\}. \textbackslash n Helpful:}
    \]
    We report the percentage of generations classified as informative.

    \item \textbf{Truthfulness $\times$ Informativeness (\%).}
    Following prior work, we report the percentage of responses that are both truthful and informative. For each prompt, this metric is computed as the product of the binary truthfulness and informativeness labels, and the final score is averaged over all prompts.

    \item \textbf{Win Rate (\%).}
    We adopt the reward-model win-rate metric used in RewardBench~\citep{lambert2025rewardbench} and ODESteer~\citep{zhaoodesteer}. Given a set of prompts $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$ and two candidate systems $A$ and $B$, let $s_i^A$ and $s_i^B$ denote their reward-model scores under the same reward model. The win rate of $A$ over $B$ is defined as
    \[
    \mathrm{Win}(A,B)
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \left[
    \mathbbm{1}(s_i^A > s_i^B)
    +
    \frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}(s_i^A = s_i^B)
    \right],
    \]
    where $\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function. A value of $0.5$ indicates parity with system $B$, values greater than $0.5$ indicate that $A$ outperforms $B$, and ties contribute $0.5$ by convention.

    \item \textbf{RM$_{\text{mean}}$ and RM$_{90}$.}
    For UltraFeedback, we use an LLM-as-a-judge reward model to score helpfulness. Specifically, we use \texttt{Skywork/Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/Skywork/Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B}}. We report RM$_{\text{mean}}$, the average reward score across examples and three random seeds, and RM$_{90}$, the 90th percentile reward score.

    \item \textbf{Toxicity Score.}
    Toxicity is evaluated using the Perspective API\footnote{\url{https://perspectiveapi.com/}}. The toxicity score is a probability-like score indicating the likelihood that a reader would perceive the generated text as toxic. Lower values therefore indicate less toxic generations.

    \item \textbf{Perplexity.}
    We evaluate generation fluency using perplexity under GPT-2-XL~\cite{radford2019language}. Given a generated response $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_T)$, GPT-2-XL assigns a next-token negative log-likelihood
    \[
    \mathcal{L}(y)
    =
    -\frac{1}{T-1}
    \sum_{t=1}^{T-1}
    \log p_{\text{GPT2-XL}}(y_{t+1}\mid y_{\leq t}).
    \]
    The perplexity of $y$ is then,
    $\mathrm{PPL}(y)
    =
    \exp\left(\mathcal{L}(y)\right).$ Lower perplexity indicates that the generated text is more fluent under GPT-2-XL.

    \item \textbf{Dist-$n$.}
    We measure lexical diversity using Dist-$n$ for $n\in\{1,2,3\}$. For a generated response $y$, let $\mathcal{G}_n(y)$ denote the multiset of all word-level $n$-grams in $y$, obtained by whitespace tokenization. Dist-$n$ is defined as
    \[
    \mathrm{Dist}\text{-}n(y)
    =
    \frac{
    \left|\mathrm{Unique}(\mathcal{G}_n(y))\right|
    }{
    \left|\mathcal{G}_n(y)\right|
    }.
    \]
    If the response contains fewer than $n$ words, we set $\mathrm{Dist}\text{-}n(y)=0$. Higher Dist-$n$ indicates greater lexical diversity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implementation Details}


\label{appendix:implementation_details}

All experiments use one H100 80GB GPU. We extend the ODESteer codebase\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ZhaoHongjue/odesteer}} to implement the steering methods and baselines. We set $\varepsilon=\sigma^2$ to the squared median geodesic distance to the fifth nearest neighbor in $\mathcal D^+\cup\mathcal D^-$, with a lower bound of $(10^{-3}R)^2$. We run five alternating Sinkhorn sweeps from $\log\boldsymbol a^+=\boldsymbol0$. The steering bandwidths follow Eq.~\eqref{eq:adaptive-bandwidth}; $b_g=\varepsilon/256$, $\kappa=20$, and $K=10$. The in-distribution configuration is ungated, with $g_q=1$ and $T=0.5$. The OOD configuration uses $p=0.7$ and $T=0.65$. Steering acts through a forward hook at layer $\ell$ on the last-token activation at every decoding step. Our ITI baseline uses the normalized logistic-probe direction implemented in this codebase.

\subsection{Empirical Validation of Spherical Geometry assumption}
\label{appendix:norm_concentration}

\Cobras models residual-stream activations at a fixed layer as lying approximately on a hypersphere. As shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:cobras}, it projects each query onto the fitted sphere (Line~\ref{alg:cobras:norm_equation}), \[ \mathbf{q}_0 = R \frac{\mathbf{h}_q}{\|\mathbf{h}_q\|}, \] performs steering on the sphere, and restores the original norm afterward. Thus, steering depends on activation direction rather than magnitude. We test this assumption by measuring the coefficient of variation (CoV) of residual-stream norms across layers and model families. For the four models used in the main experiments, we use 5,918 TruthfulQA activations; for four additional architectures (Gemma-2-2B~\cite{team2024gemma}, GPT-OSS-20B~\cite{agarwal2025gpt}, LLama-3.2-1B, Qwen2.5-1.5B), we use approximately 110K tokens of web text.

Overall, these results support the hyperspherical approximation in the layers and positions where \Cobras is applied, while identifying the initial attention-sink token as a clear exception.

\subsection{Effect of steering strength $T$ on \Cobras} 
\label{appendix:cobras_vs_t}

Figure~\ref{fig:cobras_vs_t} shows that steering strength has a non-monotonic effect. On TruthfulQA, moderate $T$ generally improves True$\times$Info, with peaks around $T=0.6$ for LLaMA3.1-8B, $T=0.7$ for Mistral-7B, and $T=0.4$ for Qwen2.5-7B; larger values often degrade performance, suggesting over-steering. Falcon-7B is flatter and weaker overall, with a sharp drop at high $T$. On UltraFeedback, the trend is more model-dependent: LLaMA3.1-8B benefits from moderate $T$, Falcon-7B is relatively stable, while Mistral-7B and Qwen2.5-7B degrade as $T$ increases. Overall, these results support using moderate steering, typically $T\approx0.5$--$0.7$ for TruthfulQA and lower-to-moderate values for UltraFeedback.

\subsection{Diversity on Detoxification}


Table~\ref{tab:detox_dist} shows the generation diversity of steering methods on the detoxification task using Dist-$n$ metrics. Overall, \Cobras preserves lexical diversity close to the original model across all four backbones. The gap is small for Mistral-7B and LLaMA3.1-8B, where Dist-2 and Dist-3 remain nearly unchanged compared to the original model.

We also observe that some baselines reduce diversity more noticeably. For example, CAA lowers diversity on Falcon-7B and Qwen2.5-7B, while SphericalSteer shows a larger drop on Qwen2.5-7B. In contrast, \Cobras maintains high Dist-2 and Dist-3 scores, suggesting that its detoxification effect does not come from generating overly repetitive or generic responses.



\subsection{Impact of geodesic Euler steps $K$}

Table~\ref{tab:cobras_truthfulqa_iters} shows the effect of increasing the number of geodesic Euler steps $K$ in our proposed \Cobras steering method on TruthfulQA. Overall, increasing $K$ improves the True $\times$ Info score across all models, although the trend differs slightly by model.

For Falcon-7B, larger $K$ increases informativeness, but also reduces the True score, leading to only a modest gain in True $\times$ Info. For Mistral-7B, performance is relatively stable across different values of $K$, with a small improvement at $K=10$. LLaMA3.1-8B benefits the most from increasing $K$, where True $\times$ Info improves consistently from $64.6$ at $K=1$ to $68.5$ at $K=10$. These results suggest that using more geodesic Euler steps generally improves steering quality, with $K=10$ giving the best overall performance in our experiments.

\subsection{Performance vs Steering Strength for baselines}

To select the steering strength for each baseline, we sweep over model-specific ranges: $T=20$--$23$ for Falcon-7B, $T=2$--$5$ for Mistral-7B, $T=3$--$6$ for LLaMA3.1-8B, and $T=13$--$16$ for Qwen2.5-7B. Figure~\ref{fig:performance_vs_t} shows the performance of RepE, ITI, CAA, and ODESteer on TruthfulQA and UltraFeedback. For baselines without a comparable steering-strength parameter, we use the best values reported in their papers.

The results show that the best steering strength depends on both the model and the method. In general, some methods improve TruthfulQA performance at moderate values of $T$, but larger steering strengths can hurt UltraFeedback performance. This trend is especially visible for ODESteer on Mistral-7B and LLaMA3.1-8B, where increasing $T$ leads to a large drop in UltraFeedback win rate. CAA is more stable on UltraFeedback and often improves with larger $T$, while ITI tends to change more gradually across the sweep.
