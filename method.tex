\section{Background}
\label{sec:bg}

\paragraph{Setup and notation.}
We fix a decoder-only transformer of residual width $d$ and a layer index $\ell$, and write
$\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ for the layer-$\ell$ residual-stream activation at the last token position.
Two populations of inputs are given, one exhibiting a desired behavior and one an undesired behavior, with
activation distributions $\pplus$ and $\pminus$ observed through i.i.d.\ samples
$\mathcal{D}^{+}=\{\boldsymbol{h}^{+}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{+}}\sim\pplus$ and
$\mathcal{D}^{-}=\{\boldsymbol{h}^{-}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{-}}\sim\pminus$, whose empirical measures we denote
$\pplushat$ and $\pminushat$. At inference, a steering map is applied to the layer-$\ell$ activation of each
decoded token, beginning with the last prompt token, and $\hquery$ denotes the activation currently being
steered. The map should move $\hquery$ toward regions of high density under $\pplus$ and low density under
$\pminus$, and it modifies no other activation. Vectors and vector fields are set in bold.

\paragraph{Spherical geometry of activations.}
Residual-stream norms concentrate tightly across inputs at a fixed layer, both
empirically~\citep{pham2024householder, you2026spherical} and as a consequence of the residual
architecture~\citep{yang2023spectral, xie2026controlled}. Figure~\ref{fig:norm-concentration} shows this
for the four models we study, for which the coefficient of variation of the layer-normalized last-token norm
lies between $2.5\%$ and $7.3\%$, and Appendix~\ref{appendix:norm_concentration} reports the same
measurement layer by layer and for four further architectures. We therefore model the state space as the
sphere $\Sph=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert=R\}$ of radius $R$, the mean norm
over $\mathcal{D}^{+}\cup\mathcal{D}^{-}$, and restore the original norm of $\hquery$ after steering, so that
the intervention changes only the direction of the activation.

\paragraph{Operations on $\Sph$.}
We write $\Pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{I}-R^{-2}\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}$ for the projector
onto the tangent space at $\boldsymbol{x}$, $\nablaS f=\Pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}\nabla f$ for the Riemannian
gradient, and $d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=R\arccos(R^{-2}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y})$
for the geodesic distance. The exponential map $\exp_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{v})$ moves from
$\boldsymbol{x}$ along the tangent vector $\boldsymbol{v}$ for a geodesic arc of length
$\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert$, and its inverse $\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})$, defined for
$\boldsymbol{y}\neq-\boldsymbol{x}$, is the tangent vector at $\boldsymbol{x}$ that points toward
$\boldsymbol{y}$ with norm $d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})$:
\begin{equation}
\exp_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{v})
= \cos\!\left(\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert}{R}\right)\boldsymbol{x}
+ R\sin\!\left(\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert}{R}\right)\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert},
\qquad
\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y}) = \frac{\theta}{\sin\theta}\,\Pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{y},
\quad \theta = \frac{d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})}{R}.
\label{eq:expmap}
\end{equation}
The steering field derived below is a weighted sum of logarithm maps toward the contrastive samples, and
each steering step is an exponential map. At zero tangent length or zero angular separation, the formulas
use their continuous limits.

\section{LLM Steering as a Schr\"{o}dinger Bridge}
\label{sec:method}

\Cobras uses contrastive activations to decide \emph{where} to steer and \emph{how much} to intervene.
It fits a transport plan from undesired to desired activations, then uses that plan to combine attraction
toward desired samples with repulsion from undesired samples near the current activation. A per-token
class score and a per-prompt support score control the strength of this direction. Geodesic updates
preserve the activation's original norm.

We first derive the continuous bridge identities that motivate this construction
(Section~\ref{subsec:sb}), then give the finite-sample direction, strength controls, and inference
procedure (Sections~\ref{subsec:estimation}--\ref{subsec:procedure}). Section~\ref{subsec:reduction}
establishes the connection to density-ratio steering.

\subsection{A transport objective and its steering direction}
\label{subsec:sb}

A Schr\"{o}dinger bridge transforms one distribution into another while minimizing its departure
from a reference stochastic process. Here the endpoints are the undesired and desired activation
distributions, and the reference $Q$ is Brownian motion on $\Sph$, started uniformly, with generator
$\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\Delta_{\Sph}$. If $P$ is a distribution over continuous trajectories and $P_t$
its marginal at time $t$, the bridge solves
\begin{equation}
P^\star=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{P}\KL(P\,\Vert\,Q)
\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad P_0=\pminus,\quad P_1=\pplus.
\label{eq:sb}
\end{equation}
Thus the endpoint constraints specify the desired population change, and relative entropy selects
the evolution closest to the reference motion. Bridge time $t\in[0,1]$ is separate from decoding time.
For this continuous derivation, assume strictly positive $C^2$ endpoint densities on $\Sph$.

The solution has two positive Schr\"{o}dinger potentials: $\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)$ propagates
information from the undesired population, and $\psi(\boldsymbol{x},t)$ propagates information from
the desired endpoint~\citep{leonard2013survey}. Their product gives the bridge density:
\begin{equation}
p_t(\boldsymbol{x})\propto\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)\psi(\boldsymbol{x},t).
\label{eq:product}
\end{equation}
The bridge drift is $\sigma^2\nablaS\log\psi$. Subtracting the diffusion contribution
$\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\nablaS\log p_t$ gives the deterministic probability flow with the same time
marginals~\citep{song2020score}:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}_t}{dt}
=\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\nablaS\log
\frac{\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)}{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)}.
\label{eq:ldr}
\end{equation}
Appendix~\ref{apx:static} gives the derivation. These identities supply the two ingredients we use:
a potential \emph{ratio} for direction and a potential \emph{product} for support.
The potentials are not the endpoint densities themselves; their relation to ordinary density-ratio
steering is established in Section~\ref{subsec:reduction}.

\subsection{Computing the direction from contrastive samples}
\label{subsec:estimation}

\paragraph{Fit the transport plan once.}
Project $\mathcal D^\pm$ onto $\Sph$ and define the affinity kernel
\begin{equation}
K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
=\exp\!\left[-\frac{d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})^2}{2b}\right],
\qquad b>0.
\label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
Here $b$ is a squared-distance scale. This kernel uses the leading short-time exponential form of
the heat kernel~\citep{chen2023logarithmic}; it is a chosen affinity, not the exact spherical
Brownian transition density. Applying entropic transport to the empirical populations gives
\begin{equation}
\pi^\star_{ji}=a_j^-a_i^+e^{-c_{ji}},
\qquad
c_{ji}=\frac{d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol h_j^-,\boldsymbol h_i^+)^2}{2\varepsilon}.
\label{eq:plan}
\end{equation}
The entry $\pi^\star_{ji}$ assigns mass from undesired sample $j$ to desired sample $i$.
The positive scalings $a_j^-,a_i^+$ enforce uniform row and column marginals, and
$\varepsilon$ controls the cost--entropy tradeoff. We fit them with Sinkhorn
updates~\citep{cuturi2013sinkhorn}; the objective and updates are in Appendix~\ref{apx:static}.

\paragraph{Use local transport connections to weight the direction.}
At an activation $\boldsymbol{x}$, a desired sample should contribute according to both its proximity
to $\boldsymbol{x}$ and its connections to nearby undesired samples. We encode the second quantity by
\begin{align}
m^+_{i,b}(\boldsymbol{x})
&=\sum_j a_j^-K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_j^-)\,e^{-c_{ji}},
\label{eq:ext-psi}\\
m^-_{j,b}(\boldsymbol{x})
&=\sum_i a_i^+K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_i^+)\,e^{-c_{ji}}.
\label{eq:ext-phi}
\end{align}
For example, $a_i^+m^+_{i,b}(\boldsymbol{x})
=\sum_j\pi^\star_{ji}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_j^-)$ is the transport mass arriving
at desired sample $i$, weighted by the source samples' proximity to $\boldsymbol{x}$.
The multiplier $m^+_{i,b}$ divides this mass by $a_i^+$; it is a reweighting coefficient,
not a conditional probability. Combining it with the desired sample's own proximity gives
\begin{equation}
\bar w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})
=\frac{m^+_{i,b}(\boldsymbol{x})K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_i^+)}
{\sum_{i'}m^+_{i',b}(\boldsymbol{x})K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_{i'}^+)},
\label{eq:weights-pos}
\end{equation}
with $\bar w_j^-$ defined symmetrically. The steering field is
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol v(\boldsymbol{x})
=\underbrace{\sum_i\bar w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol h_i^+)}_{\text{attraction}}
-\underbrace{\sum_j\bar w_j^-(\boldsymbol{x})\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol h_j^-)}_{\text{repulsion}}.
\label{eq:cobras-field}
\end{equation}
Each logarithm map is a tangent vector toward a sample. Its weight depends on distances within
both populations and the fitted connections between them.

The link to Eq.~\eqref{eq:ldr} is the kernel identity
$b\nablaS\log\sum_i m_iK_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_i)
=\sum_i w_i\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol h_i)$ for fixed positive coefficients $m_i$
(Appendix~\ref{app:grad-psi}). \Cobras recomputes the conditional multipliers at each iterate and
holds them fixed when forming this direction. This gives a gradient of a locally defined potential
ratio; differentiating through the multipliers would define a different field
(Appendix~\ref{apx:omitted-term}).

We use one fixed bandwidth $b$ here to make the construction and its density-ratio limit explicit.
The implementation uses a separate distance-based bandwidth for each population and current
activation, as specified in Appendix~\ref{apx:bandwidth}. Neither choice assigns a bridge time to
the token, so the stationary conditional field does not inherit the continuous bridge's
endpoint-matching guarantee.

\subsection{Controlling when and how much to steer}
\label{subsec:strength}

We normalize $\boldsymbol v$ before each update. Two additional factors control its strength:
a per-token side factor $s$ reduces steering on the desired side of the class boundary,
and a per-prompt support gate $g$ reduces steering on unfamiliar inputs.

\paragraph{Side factor.}
Following the antipodal von Mises--Fisher (vMF) class model used in spherical
steering~\citep{you2026spherical}, let $\bar{\boldsymbol h}^{\pm}$ be the means of the
projected samples and define
\begin{align}
\boldsymbol\mu
&=\frac{\bar{\boldsymbol h}^+-\bar{\boldsymbol h}^-}
{\|\bar{\boldsymbol h}^+-\bar{\boldsymbol h}^-\|},
\label{eq:mu}\\
s(\boldsymbol{x})
&=\left(1+e^{2\kappa\langle\boldsymbol{x}/R,\boldsymbol\mu\rangle}\right)^{-1}.
\label{eq:side}
\end{align}
Under equal class priors and shared concentration $\kappa$, this is the undesired-class
posterior: the vMF normalizers cancel. We use it directly as a smooth attenuation factor.
It equals $1/2$ at the boundary and decreases on the desired side.

\paragraph{Support gate.}
A class posterior can remain confident far from both populations. To measure proximity to the
contrastive data, extend the fitted Sinkhorn scalings into two kernel factors:
\begin{equation}
\widehat\varphi_b(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_j a_j^-K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_j^-),
\qquad
\widehat\psi_b(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_i a_i^+K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_i^+).
\label{eq:frozen-factors}
\end{equation}
Motivated by Eq.~\eqref{eq:product}, use their product at bandwidth $b_g$ as an unnormalized
support score:
\begin{equation}
\hat p_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})
=\widehat\varphi_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})\widehat\psi_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x}),
\qquad
u(\boldsymbol{x})=-\log\hat p_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x}).
\label{eq:support-score}
\end{equation}
Large $u$ indicates low support. This score is a kernel proxy, not an exact bridge marginal.

We calibrate $u$ on in-distribution last-prompt-token activations
$\mathcal D_{\mathrm{ref}}$, held out from evaluation and projected onto $\Sph$.
For $n=|\mathcal D_{\mathrm{ref}}|$, set
\begin{equation}
r(\boldsymbol{x})
=\frac{|\{\boldsymbol z\in\mathcal D_{\mathrm{ref}}:u(\boldsymbol z)<u(\boldsymbol{x})\}|}{n},
\qquad
g(\boldsymbol{x})=\min\!\left\{1,\frac{1-r(\boldsymbol{x})}{1-p}\right\}.
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
The coverage parameter $p\in(0,1)$ sets the reference percentile up to which the gate is one.
Above it, the gate decreases linearly to zero when the score exceeds all reference scores.
Calibration uses prompts because that is where the gate is evaluated; contrastive
question--answer activations need not have the same score distribution. It requires no OOD
examples. Appendix~\ref{apx:ood-gate} gives finite-sample calibration properties and measured
OOD separation; calibration alone does not guarantee OOD detection.

\subsection{Applying the intervention during generation}
\label{subsec:procedure}

For each incoming token activation $\hquery$, set
$\boldsymbol{x}_0=R\hquery/\|\hquery\|$ and evaluate $s(\boldsymbol{x}_0)$.
Compute the support gate $g_q$ once on the last prompt token and cache it throughout generation.
With steering strength $T\ge0$ and $K$ geodesic Euler steps, use
\begin{equation}
\Delta=\frac{\pi}{2}T\,s(\boldsymbol{x}_0)g_q\frac{R}{K},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}
=\exp_{\boldsymbol{x}_k}\!\left(
\Delta\frac{\boldsymbol v(\boldsymbol{x}_k)}{\|\boldsymbol v(\boldsymbol{x}_k)\|}
\right).
\label{eq:step}
\end{equation}
Recompute the direction at every iterate, keeping $s$ and $g_q$ fixed within the token's
integration. A zero field produces no update; $g_q=0$ leaves the whole prompt's generation
unsteered. The ungated configuration sets $g_q=1$.
The total path length is at most $\tfrac{\pi}{2}TR$, so $T$ measures the steering budget in
quarter-great-circle units. Return $\|\hquery\|\boldsymbol{x}_K/R$ to restore the original norm.

The transport costs, scalings, class axis, and calibration scores are computed offline.
Online, each dense field evaluation costs
$O(N_-N_+ + d(N_-+N_+))$, and each steered token uses $K$ evaluations.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:cobras} gives the procedure with adaptive bandwidths;
Appendix~\ref{appendix:implementation_details} lists the experimental settings.

\subsection{Connection to density-ratio steering}
\label{subsec:reduction}

Several steering directions can be understood through the objective
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol v(\boldsymbol{x})\propto
\nabla\log\frac{\pplus(\boldsymbol{x})}{\pminus(\boldsymbol{x})}.
\label{eq:ldr-ideal}
\end{equation}
For equal isotropic Gaussian covariances, this gradient is the difference of class means
used by CAA~\citep{rimsky2024steering}. ODESteer fits the log-ratio directly and follows
its state-dependent gradient~\citep{zhaoodesteer}. Appendix~\ref{apx:ldr-formalize}
compares these directional interpretations and their limits.

For \Cobras, ordinary kernel density-ratio steering appears when the transport costs vanish.
At a fixed evaluation bandwidth $b$, define
\begin{equation}
\hat p_b^\pm(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{N_\pm}\sum_k K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_k^\pm),
\qquad
\boldsymbol v_b^{\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})
=b\nablaS\log\frac{\hat p_b^+(\boldsymbol{x})}{\hat p_b^-(\boldsymbol{x})}.
\label{eq:ldr-kde}
\end{equation}
The kernel's common density normalizer cancels in this ratio. As $\varepsilon\to\infty$,
the fitted coupling approaches the independent plan and the conditional multipliers become
uniform within each population. The following bound makes the field reduction precise.

\begin{proposition}[Reduction to the kernel density-ratio field]
\label{prop:ldr-limit}
Fix the projected samples and $b>0$. For any positive scalings in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ext-psi}--\eqref{eq:ext-phi}, let $c_{\max}=\max_{j,i}c_{ji}$.
Away from the samples' antipodes,
\begin{equation}
\|\boldsymbol v(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol v_b^{\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})\|
\le 2\pi R(e^{c_{\max}}-1).
\label{eq:prop1-bound}
\end{equation}
Since $c_{\max}\le\pi^2R^2/(2\varepsilon)$, the unnormalized field converges uniformly
to $\boldsymbol v_b^{\mathrm{kde}}$ as $\varepsilon\to\infty$ at fixed samples and bandwidth.
\end{proposition}

The proof in Appendix~\ref{apx:proof-ldr-limit} needs only positivity of the scalings, so the
bound also covers finitely many Sinkhorn iterations. It establishes an $O(1/\varepsilon)$
limit, not accuracy at the experimental transport scale. It also does not establish population
density estimation or convergence of the normalized direction near zeros of the limiting field.
Thus the theory connects the conditional construction to a familiar objective while keeping
the guarantees distinct from the token-level intervention.
