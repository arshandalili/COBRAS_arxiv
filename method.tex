\section{Background}
\label{sec:bg}

\paragraph{Setup and notation.}
We fix a decoder-only transformer of residual width $d$ and a layer index $\ell$, and write
$\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ for the layer-$\ell$ residual-stream activation at the last token position.
Two populations of inputs are given, one exhibiting a desired behavior and one an undesired behavior, with
activation distributions $\pplus$ and $\pminus$ observed through i.i.d.\ samples
$\mathcal{D}^{+}=\{\boldsymbol{h}^{+}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{+}}\sim\pplus$ and
$\mathcal{D}^{-}=\{\boldsymbol{h}^{-}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{-}}\sim\pminus$, whose empirical measures we denote
$\pplushat$ and $\pminushat$. At inference, a steering map is applied to the layer-$\ell$ activation of each
decoded token, beginning with the last prompt token, and $\hquery$ denotes the activation currently being
steered. The map should move $\hquery$ toward regions of high density under $\pplus$ and low density under
$\pminus$, and it modifies no other activation. Vectors and vector fields are set in bold.

\paragraph{Spherical geometry of activations.}
Residual-stream norms concentrate tightly across inputs at a fixed layer, both
empirically~\citep{pham2024householder, you2026spherical} and as a consequence of the residual
architecture~\citep{yang2023spectral, xie2026controlled}. Figure~\ref{fig:norm-concentration} shows this
for the four models we study, for which the coefficient of variation of the layer-normalized last-token norm
lies between $2.5\%$ and $7.3\%$, and Appendix~\ref{appendix:norm_concentration} reports the same
measurement layer by layer and for four further architectures. We therefore model the state space as the
sphere $\Sph=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert=R\}$ of radius $R$, the mean norm
over $\mathcal{D}^{+}\cup\mathcal{D}^{-}$, and restore the original norm of $\hquery$ after steering, so that
the intervention changes only the direction of the activation.

\paragraph{Operations on $\Sph$.}
We write $\Pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{I}-R^{-2}\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}$ for the projector
onto the tangent space at $\boldsymbol{x}$, $\nablaS f=\Pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}\nabla f$ for the Riemannian
gradient, and $d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=R\arccos(R^{-2}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y})$
for the geodesic distance. The exponential map $\exp_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{v})$ moves from
$\boldsymbol{x}$ along the tangent vector $\boldsymbol{v}$ for a geodesic arc of length
$\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert$, and its inverse $\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})$, defined for
$\boldsymbol{y}\neq-\boldsymbol{x}$, is the tangent vector at $\boldsymbol{x}$ that points toward
$\boldsymbol{y}$ with norm $d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})$:
\begin{equation}
\exp_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{v})
= \cos\!\left(\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert}{R}\right)\boldsymbol{x}
+ R\sin\!\left(\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert}{R}\right)\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert},
\qquad
\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y}) = \frac{\theta}{\sin\theta}\,\Pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{y},
\quad \theta = \frac{d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})}{R}.
\label{eq:expmap}
\end{equation}
The steering field derived below is a weighted sum of logarithm maps toward the contrastive samples, and
each steering step is an exponential map. At zero tangent length or zero angular separation, the formulas
use their continuous limits.

\section{LLM Steering as a Schr\"{o}dinger Bridge}
\label{sec:method}

In this section, we first cast steering as a transportation problem and then derive our method, \Cobras. We first derive the continuous bridge identities that motivate this construction (Section~\ref{subsec:sb}), then give the finite-sample direction, strength controls, and inference procedure (Sections~\ref{subsec:estimation}--\ref{subsec:procedure}). Section~\ref{subsec:reduction} establishes the connection to density-ratio steering.

\subsection{A transport objective and its steering direction}
\label{subsec:sb}

A Schr\"{o}dinger bridge describes how one distribution evolves into another while staying as close as possible to a reference random motion. Here the reference $Q$ is Brownian motion on $\Sph$, which moves randomly along the sphere with diffusion scale $\sigma^2$, starting from a uniform distribution. We seek a distribution $P$ over continuous trajectories that carries the undesired population $\pminus$ to the desired population $\pplus$. Writing $P_t$ for the activation distribution at time $t$, the bridge solves
\begin{equation}
P^\star=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{P}\KL(P\,\Vert\,Q)
\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad P_0=\pminus,\quad P_1=\pplus.
\label{eq:sb}
\end{equation}
Thus the endpoint constraints specify the desired population change, and relative entropy selects the evolution closest to the reference motion. The time variable $t\in[0,1]$ indexes this population transport independently of token positions in the generated sequence.

The solution is characterized by two positive Schr\"{o}dinger potentials, $\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)$ and $\psi(\boldsymbol{x},t)$, which propagate information from the undesired and desired endpoints, respectively~\citep{leonard2013survey}. Together they determine the bridge density through their product,
\begin{equation}
p_t(\boldsymbol{x})\propto\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)\psi(\boldsymbol{x},t).
\label{eq:product}
\end{equation}
Starting from $\pminus$, a deterministic flow can reproduce the bridge's activation distribution at every time $t$~\citep{song2020score}. Its direction follows the gradient of the log-ratio of the two potentials,
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}_t}{dt}
=\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\nablaS\log
\frac{\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)}{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}_t,t)}.
\label{eq:ldr}
\end{equation}
These identities, derived in Appendix~\ref{apx:static}, motivate a potential \emph{ratio} for the steering direction and a potential \emph{product} for support. Since the potentials describe the intermediate bridge rather than the endpoint densities, the connection to ordinary density-ratio steering requires the analysis of it in the limit, as done in Section~\ref{subsec:reduction}.

\subsection{Computing the direction from contrastive samples}
\label{subsec:estimation}

In this section, we focus on determining the steering direction using the ratio of the potentials.

\paragraph{Fit the transport plan once.}
Project $\mathcal D^\pm$ onto $\Sph$ and define the affinity kernel
\begin{equation}
K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
=\exp\!\left[-\frac{d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})^2}{2b}\right],
\qquad b>0.
\label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
The bandwidth $b$ sets the squared-distance scale of this affinity, whose form is motivated by the leading short-time exponential behavior of the heat kernel~\citep{chen2023logarithmic}. Using it as a surrogate for the spherical Brownian transition density in a discrete entropic transport problem gives the plan
\begin{equation}
\pi^\star_{ji}=a_j^-a_i^+e^{-c_{ji}},
\qquad
c_{ji}=\frac{d_{\Sph}(\boldsymbol h_j^-,\boldsymbol h_i^+)^2}{2\varepsilon}.
\label{eq:plan}
\end{equation}
The entry $\pi^\star_{ji}$ assigns mass from undesired sample $j$ to desired sample $i$, with $\varepsilon$ controlling the cost--entropy tradeoff. The positive scalings $a_j^-,a_i^+$ enforce uniform row and column marginals and are fitted using Sinkhorn updates~\citep{cuturi2013sinkhorn}, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{apx:static}.

\paragraph{Use local transport connections to weight the direction.}
At an activation $\boldsymbol{x}$, a desired sample should contribute according to both its proximity to $\boldsymbol{x}$ and its connections to nearby undesired samples. We encode the second quantity by
\begin{align}
m^+_{i,b}(\boldsymbol{x})
&=\sum_j a_j^-K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_j^-)\,e^{-c_{ji}},
\label{eq:ext-psi}\\
m^-_{j,b}(\boldsymbol{x})
&=\sum_i a_i^+K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_i^+)\,e^{-c_{ji}}.
\label{eq:ext-phi}
\end{align}
For example, $a_i^+m^+_{i,b}(\boldsymbol{x}) =\sum_j\pi^\star_{ji}K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_j^-)$ is the transport mass arriving at desired sample $i$, weighted by the source samples' proximity to $\boldsymbol{x}$. We divide this mass by $a_i^+$ to obtain the reweighting coefficient $m^+_{i,b}$, then combine it with the desired sample's own proximity to form the normalized weight
\begin{equation}
\bar w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})
=\frac{m^+_{i,b}(\boldsymbol{x})K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_i^+)}
{\sum_{i'}m^+_{i',b}(\boldsymbol{x})K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_{i'}^+)},
\label{eq:weights-pos}
\end{equation}
with $\bar w_j^-$ defined symmetrically. The steering field is
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol v(\boldsymbol{x})
=\underbrace{\sum_i\bar w_i^+(\boldsymbol{x})\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol h_i^+)}_{\text{attraction}}
-\underbrace{\sum_j\bar w_j^-(\boldsymbol{x})\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol h_j^-)}_{\text{repulsion}}.
\label{eq:cobras-field}
\end{equation}
Each logarithm map is a tangent vector toward a sample. Its weight depends on distances within both populations and the fitted connections between them.

The link to Eq.~\eqref{eq:ldr} is the kernel identity $b\nablaS\log\sum_i m_iK_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_i) =\sum_i w_i\log_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol h_i)$ for fixed positive coefficients $m_i$ (Appendix~\ref{app:grad-psi}). \Cobras applies this identity with the conditional multipliers evaluated and held fixed at each iterate, giving the gradient of a locally defined potential ratio. Differentiating through the multipliers would introduce the additional terms derived in Appendix~\ref{apx:omitted-term} and therefore produce a different field.

We present the construction at a fixed bandwidth $b$ to make its density-ratio limit explicit, while the implementation selects a separate distance-based bandwidth for each population and current activation (Appendix~\ref{apx:bandwidth}). Because the resulting stationary field does not assign a bridge time to the token, it does not inherit the continuous bridge's endpoint-matching guarantee.

\subsection{Controlling when and how much to steer}
\label{subsec:strength}

Normalizing $\boldsymbol v$ before each update separates its direction from the intervention strength. We scale each token's update by a steering multiplier $s$, which decreases as the activation aligns with the desired direction, and a support gate $g$, which reduces steering on unfamiliar prompts.

\paragraph{Steering multiplier.}
We measure alignment with the desired direction using the normalized difference between the desired and undesired mean activations. Let $\bar{\boldsymbol h}^{\pm}$ be the means of the projected samples. Following the attenuation rule used in spherical steering~\citep{you2026spherical}, we define
\begin{align}
\boldsymbol\mu
&=\frac{\bar{\boldsymbol h}^+-\bar{\boldsymbol h}^-}
{\|\bar{\boldsymbol h}^+-\bar{\boldsymbol h}^-\|},
\label{eq:mu}\\
s(\boldsymbol{x})
&=\left(1+e^{2\kappa\langle\boldsymbol{x}/R,\boldsymbol\mu\rangle}\right)^{-1}.
\label{eq:side}
\end{align}
The inner product measures alignment with $\boldsymbol\mu$, and $\kappa>0$ controls how sharply the multiplier changes. At zero alignment, $s=1/2$. As alignment with $\boldsymbol\mu$ increases, $s$ decreases toward zero, reducing the intervention on activations already oriented in the desired direction. Appendix~\ref{apx:pseudocode} gives a probabilistic interpretation of this rule.

\paragraph{Support gate.}
Alignment with $\boldsymbol\mu$ alone does not establish proximity to the contrastive data. To assess this proximity, we extend the fitted Sinkhorn scalings into two kernel factors,
\begin{equation}
\widehat\varphi_b(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_j a_j^-K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_j^-),
\qquad
\widehat\psi_b(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_i a_i^+K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_i^+).
\label{eq:frozen-factors}
\end{equation}
Following the product form of the bridge density in Eq.~\eqref{eq:product}, we define an unnormalized support score at bandwidth $b_g$,
\begin{equation}
\hat p_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})
=\widehat\varphi_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x})\widehat\psi_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x}),
\qquad
u(\boldsymbol{x})=-\log\hat p_{b_g}(\boldsymbol{x}).
\label{eq:support-score}
\end{equation}
At the chosen bandwidth, this product serves as a kernel proxy for the bridge marginal, with larger values of its negative logarithm $u$ indicating lower support.

We calibrate $u$ using only in-distribution prompt activations $\mathcal D_{\mathrm{ref}}$, held out from evaluation and projected onto $\Sph$. Taking these activations at the steering layer and last prompt position matches the inputs encountered by the gate during generation, since contrastive question--answer activations may follow a different score distribution. For $n=|\mathcal D_{\mathrm{ref}}|$, we rank a query's score against the reference scores and convert its rank into the gate
\begin{equation}
r(\boldsymbol{x})
=\frac{|\{\boldsymbol z\in\mathcal D_{\mathrm{ref}}:u(\boldsymbol z)<u(\boldsymbol{x})\}|}{n},
\qquad
g(\boldsymbol{x})=\min\!\left\{1,\frac{1-r(\boldsymbol{x})}{1-p}\right\}.
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
With coverage parameter $p\in(0,1)$, the gate applies full strength up to the reference $p$-quantile and then decreases linearly with rank, reaching zero for scores above all reference scores. Appendix~\ref{apx:ood-gate} explains how $p$ controls in-distribution steering strength and evaluates how well the support scores separate OOD prompts.

\subsection{Applying the intervention during generation}
\label{subsec:procedure}

For each incoming token activation $\hquery$, set $\boldsymbol{x}_0=R\hquery/\|\hquery\|$ and evaluate $s(\boldsymbol{x}_0)$. The prompt's support gate $g_q$ is computed once on its last token and cached throughout generation, with $g_q=1$ in the ungated configuration. Using this gate and a steering strength $T\ge0$, we integrate the direction over $K$ geodesic Euler steps,
\begin{equation}
\Delta=\frac{\pi}{2}T\,s(\boldsymbol{x}_0)g_q\frac{R}{K},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}
=\exp_{\boldsymbol{x}_k}\!\left(
\Delta\frac{\boldsymbol v(\boldsymbol{x}_k)}{\|\boldsymbol v(\boldsymbol{x}_k)\|}
\right).
\label{eq:step}
\end{equation}
During each token's integration, we recompute the direction at every iterate while keeping $s$ and $g_q$ fixed, then return $\|\hquery\|\boldsymbol{x}_K/R$ to restore the original norm. The total path length is at most $\tfrac{\pi}{2}TR$, so $T$ measures the steering budget in quarter-great-circle units. A zero field produces no update, while $g_q=0$ disables steering throughout the prompt's generation.

After computing the transport costs, scalings, mean-difference direction, and calibration scores offline, each steered token requires $K$ field evaluations at a cost of $O(N_-N_+ + d(N_-+N_+))$ per evaluation. Algorithm~\ref{alg:cobras} summarizes the adaptive-bandwidth procedure, with experimental settings given in Appendix~\ref{appendix:implementation_details}.

\subsection{Connection to density-ratio steering}
\label{subsec:reduction}

Several steering directions can be understood through the objective \begin{equation} \boldsymbol v(\boldsymbol{x})\propto \nabla\log\frac{\pplus(\boldsymbol{x})}{\pminus(\boldsymbol{x})}. \label{eq:ldr-ideal} \end{equation} For Gaussians with identity covariance, this gradient is the difference between the desired and undesired means used by CAA~\citep{rimsky2024steering}. ODESteer fits the log-ratio directly and follows its state-dependent gradient~\citep{zhaoodesteer}, as discussed alongside other parameterizations in Appendix~\ref{apx:ldr-formalize}.

For \Cobras, ordinary kernel density-ratio steering appears when the transport costs vanish. At a fixed evaluation bandwidth $b$, define \begin{equation} \hat p_b^\pm(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{N_\pm}\sum_k K_b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol h_k^\pm), \qquad \boldsymbol v_b^{\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x}) =b\nablaS\log\frac{\hat p_b^+(\boldsymbol{x})}{\hat p_b^-(\boldsymbol{x})}. \label{eq:ldr-kde} \end{equation} The kernel's common density normalizer cancels in this ratio. As $\varepsilon\to\infty$, the fitted coupling approaches the independent plan and the conditional multipliers become uniform within each population. The following bound makes the field reduction precise.

\begin{proposition}[Reduction to the kernel density-ratio field]
\label{prop:ldr-limit}
Fix the projected samples and $b>0$. For any positive scalings in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ext-psi}--\eqref{eq:ext-phi}, let $c_{\max}=\max_{j,i}c_{ji}$.
Away from the samples' antipodes,
\begin{equation}
\|\boldsymbol v(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol v_b^{\mathrm{kde}}(\boldsymbol{x})\|
\le 2\pi R(e^{c_{\max}}-1).
\label{eq:prop1-bound}
\end{equation}
Since $c_{\max}\le\pi^2R^2/(2\varepsilon)$, the unnormalized field converges uniformly
to $\boldsymbol v_b^{\mathrm{kde}}$ as $\varepsilon\to\infty$ at fixed samples and bandwidth.
\end{proposition}

The proof in Appendix~\ref{apx:proof-ldr-limit} uses only positivity of the scalings, so the $O(1/\varepsilon)$ limit also covers finitely many Sinkhorn iterations. This fixed-sample comparison concerns transport reweighting, with population-density consistency requiring a separate statistical argument. Convergence of normalized directions additionally requires control near zeros of the limiting field, and the asymptotic rate need not yield a useful numerical bound at the experimental transport scale.
